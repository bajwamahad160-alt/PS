# Chapter One: The Bindery

The radio in the kitchen was already going when Owen came down the stairs.

"...and the new Light Grandmaster was back at Harbor Arena last night,
folks, for her very first exhibition. Sixteen years old and five months on
the job, and I don't mind telling you, when Talia Reyes lit up that arena I
had to take my hat off to shade my eyes. And afterward, when we asked her
who that one was for, she looked right into the microphone and said, *'Cable
Street.'* How about that..."

"Turn it up!" Tess said.

Owen reached over and turned it off.

"*Hey!*"

"I'm eating."

His sister glared at him. She was ten, sitting cross-legged on a kitchen
chair with her trading cards spread over the table in neat rows, like she
was running a bank. The seven Grandmasters had the middle of the table to
themselves. The newest card sat right in the center, still shiny, with no
bent corners at all. It showed a girl with her dark hair tied back in a high
ponytail and her hand raised, light pouring off her fingers. Across the
bottom, in gold letters: TALIA REYES, LIGHT GRANDMASTER.

And under that, in blue ink and slightly smudged, in handwriting Owen had
been reading off notes passed in class since they were seven: *To Tess, my
number one fan. Don't trade me. —T.*

It had come in the post two months ago, in a stiff Ministry envelope with
Tess's name on the front. There had been nothing in it for Owen. There had
never been anything for Owen.

"I'm never trading her," Tess said, as though he had suggested it. "Not even
for Rourke."

"Nobody asked you to trade her."

"People ask me *every day.*"

Pop was at the stove with his sleeves rolled up and his work shirt already
buttoned for the early shift at the Eastside power station. He snapped his
fingers over the burner, and a flame the size of a match flicked to life and
caught the gas. It was the same small, tidy flame he'd made every morning of
Owen's life, the same one he fed into the boilers at the station eight hours
a day, six days a week. His griamore sat on the windowsill with its cloth
cover faded from red to a tired pink. It was thin. Most people's were.

"There he is," Pop said. "Sixteen. How's it feel?"

"Same as fifteen."

"Give it till lunch." Pop slid a plate across the table: one fried egg and a
bun with a candle stuck in it. "Your mother left that before her shift. Says
happy birthday, says don't be a smart mouth at the Bindery, says wear the
good shirt."

"I'm wearing the good shirt."

Pop looked at the shirt. "That's the good shirt?"

"It's the one without the hole."

Tess was still watching him over her cards. "Do you think Talia will come?
To the Bindery? For your day?"

"Why would she?"

It came out flat and hard, and the kitchen went quiet. Tess blinked at him.
Pop glanced over from the stove and didn't say anything, which was worse.

Owen looked down at his egg.

"Light the candle," Tess said after a moment, quieter. "You have to make a
wish. Everyone makes a wish on Bindery day. Talia made a wish."

Owen knew. He'd been sitting right there when she made it.

Five months ago, in this same kitchen, on Talia's sixteenth birthday, she'd
sat in the chair where Owen was sitting now, and Pop had lit a candle in a
bun for her too, because her own dad worked nights and somebody had to.
She'd squeezed her eyes shut and wished so hard her nose wrinkled.
Afterward Owen had asked what she'd wished for, and she'd grabbed his wrist
under the table and whispered it, like it was a secret between the two of
them.

*Something that gets us out of here.*

*Us,* she'd said. Not *me.*

Then she'd gone up the hill to the Bindery and come back the Light
Grandmaster, and it turned out the wish only had room for one.

Pop reached over and snapped his fingers above the bun. The candle caught.

Owen looked at it. Everybody knew the odds. Seven Grandmaster books in the
whole world, and a Grandmaster book only came free when its holder died. The
old Light Grandmaster had passed away last winter, at ninety-one, in his
sleep. For three months after that, every sixteen-year-old in the country
had gone to the Bindery holding their breath. Then one Tuesday in the
spring, a girl from Cable Street had put her hand on a blank book, and that
had been that.

And now it was happening all over again. Six weeks ago, the Thunder
Grandmaster, a gruff old man named Barnaby Holt, had died in his armchair
during a rainstorm, which the papers all agreed was very fitting. His book
was sitting blank somewhere up the hill, waiting, and every sixteen-year-old
in the country was holding their breath again.

But you didn't wish for Grandmaster. You wished for something good and
steady, the kind of book people could build a life on: fire for the power
station, water for the canals and the fire brigade, earth for the
construction crews. Or one of the hundred other kinds of books people got
every day. Strength for the docks. Healing for the hospital. Sound, if you
wanted to work in radio. Metal, if you wanted to work anywhere at all,
because the foundries were always hiring.

What Owen wanted was harder to say out loud, even inside his own head. He
didn't need Grandmaster. He just wanted something that was *his.* Something
nobody could look straight through.

He blew out the candle and didn't say what he'd wished for.

"Did you wish for Thunder?" Tess demanded. "You wished for Thunder.
Everyone's wishing for Thunder. You can't get it, though. Talia already got
Light, and Pop says lightning doesn't strike the same street twice."

"I said that one time," said Pop.

"You said it at *dinner*."

"Eat your breakfast, Tess."

"I already ate my breakfast. I'm doing inventory."

---

Owen took the tram up the hill by himself.

He swung up onto the back rail without paying, like always, and stood with
the wind in his face while Port Aldren rolled past: the fish markets and the
cannery smokestacks, the big cranes at the harbor where the earth crews and
the strength crews lifted cargo off freighters by hand, the laundry lines
strung between tenements. Farther up, the city climbed the hill. The
buildings got taller and cleaner there, with electric signs in the windows
and motorcars shining at the curbs. At the very top, the Ministry's white
towers stood with a government airship moored at the mast, its silver belly
catching the morning sun.

Talia lived up there now. Somewhere in those towers she had rooms of her
own, and a trainer, and a handler, and a schedule written out by the hour.

Owen didn't know which window was hers. She'd never told him. She'd never
told him anything, not since the day at the Bindery. Not one letter in five
months. Not one visit. Not one word, even though she only lived at the top
of the same hill, and the tram went there every twenty minutes, and she
knew exactly which window on Cable Street was his, because she'd climbed
through it about a thousand times.

At the corner of Market Street, the tram passed a warehouse with a poster
on the side of it twenty feet tall. It was Talia, the same picture as on
Tess's card: hand raised, light pouring off her fingers, her face calm and
serious in a way Owen had never once seen it look in real life. Under her,
in huge letters: THE LIGHT OF PORT ALDREN.

Somebody had drawn a mustache on her.

Owen looked away.

He'd done it in the second month, on the night he finally understood she
wasn't going to write. He'd climbed the fire escape with a stick of harbor
chalk in his pocket, and he'd felt good about it for about a minute. Then
he'd felt stupid, and then he'd felt worse, and the rain hadn't washed it
off the way he'd been counting on.

He hadn't told anyone. He was fairly sure he never would.

The tram ground its way up the hill, and the Bindery came into view.

---

It was the biggest building Owen had ever been inside. The whole place was
white marble, cold and echoing, with brass rails that split the floor into
long lines and a ceiling painted with the seven Grandmaster marks in gold
leaf: flame, water, air, earth, light, ice, and thunder, so high up you had
to lean back to see them. The Light mark had been freshly regilded this
spring, and it shone brighter than the others. Under the Thunder mark,
somebody had hung a long black ribbon for mourning, and it stirred a little
in the draft from the doors. A big clock hung on the wall behind every
clerk's desk, and all of them ticked together, so the whole hall had a slow,
steady pulse to it.

The line was full of sixteen-year-olds and their mothers. Owen's mother was
at the cannery and his father was at the station, because you didn't miss a
shift for a Bindery day unless you could afford to, and the Calders
couldn't. It didn't matter. Plenty of kids came alone. He found the end of
the line and stood in it.

A sign at the front said HAVE YOUR BIRTH CERTIFICATE READY in letters two
feet tall. A man with a mop was leaning against the wall by the door,
looking bored.

It went slowly. At the front of each line was a desk, at each desk was a
clerk in a green visor, and in front of each clerk was a stack of blank
books: plain grey cloth, all the same. You gave your name. You put your hand
on a book. And then something happened.

Owen had been to watch before, and none of it surprised him. It was the same
every year. A girl two lines over put her hand down and her book went hot
and smoked a little, and a red mark inked itself onto the cover, and her
whole family cheered, because fire was the power station. A tall boy got
water, and his book soaked through and dripped on the marble, and the man
with the mop sighed and went over to it. A heavyset kid got strength and
accidentally lifted the front of the desk an inch off the floor, and the
clerk said "*Put it down, please,*" without even looking up. A small girl
got healing, and a boy right after her got sound and said "Oh!" so loud it
rattled the windows, and then clapped both hands over his mouth, horrified,
while everybody laughed.

Nobody made a fuss over any of it. A book was a book. A mark was a mark. It
was a good day, and a normal one.

Owen stood in line and tried very hard not to remember the last time he'd
been here.

He remembered anyway.

He'd skipped school to come up with Talia that day, five months ago. She'd
been so nervous she'd held on to his sleeve the whole time they waited, and
when the clerk called her name she'd let go of it one finger at a time.
She'd put her hand on the cover, and nothing had happened for a second. Then
the gold Light mark on the ceiling above them, eighty feet up, had started
to *glow*, and a clean white light had poured down out of it and filled the
whole hall, every corner, so bright that for a moment there were no shadows
anywhere.

Everybody in the building had gone silent. Then everybody in the building
had started shouting at once, and Owen had been shoving his way through the
crowd toward her before he'd even decided to, grinning so wide his face
hurt, because it was *Talia,* it was his Talia, and she'd done it, she'd
actually done it, and he was going to be the first person to tell her so.

He'd gotten there. He'd gotten right up to the desk, close enough to touch
her sleeve, and he'd said her name.

And she'd looked at him.

She'd looked right at him, for one second, with an expression on her face he
had never seen before and couldn't name. Then she'd looked away, as if he
were a stranger in the crowd, and she'd let the men in grey suits turn her
around by the shoulders, and she'd walked right past him. Close enough that
her sleeve brushed his arm. She hadn't said a word. She hadn't looked back.

Owen had stood there with his hand still half raised while the men walked
her out through the bronze doors and put her in a motorcar and drove her up
the hill. Everybody else in the hall had been watching the motorcar. Nobody
had been watching him, which was the only good thing about it.

That was five months ago. He hadn't heard her voice since, except on the
radio.

"*Next,*" called the clerk at the front of the line. The line shuffled
forward. Owen shuffled with it.

Behind him, somebody gasped. Then somebody else did. Then the whole back half
of the hall went quiet, and a murmur started at the doors and rolled forward
like a wave: *it's her, it's her, that's the Light Grandmaster.*

Owen didn't turn around.

He didn't have to. He could hear her coming across the marble floor, fast,
the way she'd always walked, like she was late for something. He could hear
a man's heavier footsteps hurrying after her, and a man's voice saying,
"Miss Reyes, *please*, the schedule." He could hear people shuffling back
out of her way. And then he could feel it, the way you feel sun on the back
of your neck, when she stopped right behind him.

"Owen," Talia said.

He looked straight ahead at the sign. HAVE YOUR BIRTH CERTIFICATE READY.

"Owen. Hey." Her voice was a little out of breath. "Happy birthday. I
wasn't sure if... I didn't know if you'd..."

He didn't say anything. He didn't turn around.

For five months he'd had whole arguments with her in his head, on the roof,
on the tram, in the dark before he fell asleep. He'd planned exactly what
he'd say to her if she ever came back. He'd had speeches. He'd won every
single one of those arguments.

And now she was right here, standing close enough that he could feel the
strange warmth coming off her like a stove, and he found he didn't want any
of the speeches. He only wanted one thing. He wanted her to know what it
felt like.

So he looked straight ahead and said nothing at all.

The hall had gone very quiet around them. People were staring. Everyone was
staring. The Light Grandmaster, in her long white coat with the gold mark on
the shoulder, standing in a Bindery line behind a skinny dock kid in a
patched shirt, and the dock kid wouldn't even turn around.

"Owen," Talia said again, much quieter. "*Please.*"

"Miss Reyes," said the man's voice behind her, low and urgent. "Twenty
minutes. The airfield. The *Prime Minister's* airfield."

"I know, Mr. Crane."

"People are *watching*, Miss Reyes."

"I *know*, Mr. Crane." A pause. Then, very quietly, to the back of Owen's
head: "I'm going to stay until you're done. You don't have to talk to me.
I'm just going to stay."

Owen stared at the sign until the letters stopped looking like words.

"*Next,*" called the clerk.

It was Owen.

---

The clerk was a thin, tired man with a pencil behind his ear and a nameplate
that said MR. PETTIBONE. He looked at Owen's certificate, looked at his
ledger, wrote a number, and slid a blank book across the desk. Just grey
cloth and blank pages, with a faint smell of dust and glue.

"Palm flat on the cover," Mr. Pettibone said. "Don't pull away, whatever it
feels like."

Nobody was watching.

Owen realized it all at once. The whole hall was still staring at Talia:
whispering, pointing, a girl two lines over trying to work up the nerve to
ask for her autograph. Even Mr. Pettibone kept glancing past Owen's shoulder
at the white coat. Owen was standing at the desk on his own Bindery day, the
one day in your whole life when everybody is supposed to be watching you,
and he was invisible. He was standing in her shadow, which was a funny thing
to be able to do with somebody made of light.

He wiped his hand on his trousers. His heart was going hard. He could feel
Talia standing a few steps behind him, not saying anything, and the clock on
the wall ticking along with all the others.

He put his hand on the book.

For about half a second, it was too much. It felt like standing too close to
the boilers at the station, and also like jumping into the harbor in
January, and also like the floor had tipped a little under his feet. It was
everything at once, far too much to hold.

Then it narrowed down, the way a crowd goes quiet when a door shuts, and all
that was left was one small, clear feeling: *weight.* As if the whole room
had leaned toward him, just slightly, the way a table leans when somebody
sits on one end.

Mr. Pettibone's pencil rolled off the ledger.

It rolled across the desk, slowly, the wrong way. The desk was flat, and if
anything it tipped toward the clerk, but the pencil rolled toward Owen
anyway, across the blotter and past the ink pot, and bumped to a stop
against the side of Owen's book. The ink in the ink pot had tilted too, all
of it leaning against the near side of the glass. It held there for the
length of a breath. Then the ink sloshed back level, and the pencil just lay
there like a pencil.

Mr. Pettibone looked at the pencil, frowned, and picked it back up.

The book under Owen's hand had gone cool and quiet, and very heavy. A mark
was inking itself onto the grey cloth, slowly, dark as a bruise. It wasn't a
flame or a wave or any of the others from the ceiling. Owen didn't recognize
it.

Behind him, very quietly, Talia drew in a breath.

Mr. Pettibone didn't know the mark either, but he didn't seem bothered. He
pulled a big, heavy registry off the shelf behind him, a book as thick as a
loaf of bread with a mark on every page, and began flipping through it. Past
fire and water and earth, past strength and healing and sound and metal and
dozens more, all the way to a thin section at the very back with a faded red
tab that said RARE.

He stopped. He looked at the page, and then at Owen's book, and then at the
page again.

"Huh," said Mr. Pettibone. Then, over his shoulder, "Mrs. Harrow? Can you
check one?"

---

Mrs. Harrow was old, older than anyone else working the floor, with white
hair pinned up tight and a pair of reading glasses on a chain around her
neck. She came down the row of desks without hurrying, put on the glasses,
and looked at the registry. Then she looked at the book.

She looked at the book for a long time.

It was, Owen would think later, a little bit too long.

"Yes," she said at last. "That's Gravity."

"Gravity?" Owen said.

"Gravity. It's rare." Mrs. Harrow took her glasses off and let them fall on
their chain. "There are a handful in the country. Maybe one every few years
in a city this size. I've been at this desk thirty-eight years and I've
stamped two." She said it simply, the way you'd tell someone the weather.
"Open it."

He did. The first page had filled. There was a single word written at the
top in dark ink, and under it a line that explained what it did, as plainly
as if it had always been printed there:

**Lighten.** *For a moment, the thing you name weighs nothing.*

He turned the page. It was blank. So was the next one. He flipped through
faster. The book was thick, much thicker than Pop's, and every page after the
first was empty.

"Is it broken?"

"No. Rare books fill slowly," Mrs. Harrow said. "The pages come when you're
ready for them." Then she looked at him with pale grey eyes, sharp as a
tailor's pins, and asked quietly, "When you touched it, what did you feel?"

Owen opened his mouth to tell her. *Too much. Hot and cold and heavy, all at
once.* But it had only lasted half a second, and it sounded stupid even in
his own head, and there was a whole line of people waiting behind him.

"Cold," he said. "And then it got heavy."

Mrs. Harrow held his eyes for one more moment. Then she nodded, once, and
picked up the big stamp and brought it down on the ledger.

"File number four-four-one-nine," she said. "Gravity. Happy birthday, Mr.
Calder."

She walked back down the row of desks, not hurrying.

Owen picked up his book. It was heavy in his hands, heavier than it looked,
and the mark on the cover was dark and strange and *his*. Nobody else in the
city had one. Nobody else in the building had even known what it was.

He turned around.

Talia was standing right there, three steps away, exactly where she'd said
she'd be. Her face was lit up, bright and open and so happy for him that it
looked like it hurt, the way it used to look when they were kids and he'd
won a race, and for one second Owen forgot the last five months completely.

"*Gravity,*" she said. "Owen. Do you know what Mr. Okafor said in physics?
Gravity moves at exactly the same speed as light. Exactly the same, nobody
knows why. So you can't..." She stopped, and swallowed, and tried again,
more carefully. "So you'd always be able to keep up. With me. If you
wanted."

She said it like a present. She said it like she'd been carrying it up the
hill the whole way here.

And Owen remembered the Bindery five months ago. Her face turning away. Her
sleeve brushing his arm as she walked past.

He looked at her for exactly one second, the way she had looked at him.

Then he looked away, as if she were a stranger in the crowd, and he walked
past her. Close enough that his sleeve brushed hers. He didn't say a word.
He didn't look back.

He heard her breath catch. He heard Mr. Crane say, "Miss Reyes, the
airfield, we *have* to..." He heard the whole hall go murmuring and
whispering behind him as he walked, and he kept his eyes on the bronze doors
and his book against his chest and he didn't stop.

It felt good for about as long as it took to cross the floor.

Then he pushed out through the doors into the sunlight, and it didn't feel
good at all. It felt exactly like it had felt the first time, only from the
other side, and he couldn't tell anymore which side was worse.

---

On the way home, he tried it, just to see. Mostly so he'd have something
else to think about.

He walked the long way, down along the docks, where the big cranes were
swinging cargo off the freighters. A gull was diving at a crate of mackerel.
Owen put his hand on the book, pointed with the other, and whispered,
"*Lighten.*"

Nothing happened.

The gull grabbed a fish and wheeled away, screaming.

Owen frowned. He'd said it right. He'd had his hand on it. He tried to
remember exactly what it had felt like at the desk, that one clear weight,
and realized that he'd been looking at the book, not at the gull. Thinking
about the word, not the thing.

*For a moment, the thing you name weighs nothing.*

The thing you *name.*

A rope was swinging loose from the nearest crane, knocking against the side
of a crate, back and forth. Owen looked at it. Really looked at it, the way
you'd look at somebody you were about to shout to across a crowded room.
*That* rope.

"*Lighten.*"

The rope stopped swinging and started to *rise.* Its loose end lifted up off
the crate and drifted upward in a lazy curl, slow and weightless, the way
seaweed floats in a tide pool.

It lasted about as long as it takes to breathe in. Then something sharp went
through Owen's head, right behind his eyes, like a headache arriving all at
once instead of slowly, and the rope dropped and knocked against the crate,
*thunk*, as if nothing had happened.

Owen sat down hard on a bollard and pressed the heel of his hand into his
eye until the throbbing faded.

Then he looked at the rope, and he started to laugh.

He'd floated a rope. *He'd* done that. Not a Grandmaster. Not anybody off a
trading card. Him, Owen Calder, from Cable Street, with a book nobody else
in the city had.

He turned around to tell someone, the way he'd have turned around to tell
her, once. There was nobody there.

He stopped laughing.

---

At home, he didn't mean to show off. He really didn't. But Tess knocked the
milk bottle off the edge of the table while she was reaching for her cards,
the way she knocked something off the table about twice a day, and before
Owen could think about it his hand was flat on the book and he was looking
right at the bottle.

"*Lighten.*"

The bottle stopped falling.

It hung a foot off the floor, tilted over sideways, turning very slowly, and
the milk that had been pouring out of its neck didn't fall either. It floated
out into the air in a long white ribbon that wobbled and curled like it was
underwater. One drop broke off the end and drifted up past Tess's nose,
perfectly round, with the kitchen light caught inside it.

Nobody breathed.

Then Owen's head gave a sharp, bright throb behind his eyes, the moment let
go, the bottle hit the floor and smashed, and milk went everywhere.

"*OWEN,*" Tess shrieked, and she wasn't mad at all. She was the happiest Owen
had ever seen her. "*DO IT AGAIN.*"

"There's milk on the floor..."

"*DO IT AGAIN!*"

Pop was standing in the doorway in his work clothes, soot still on his hands
from the station. He looked at the broken bottle. He looked at the book. He
looked at his son for a long moment, and something went across his face that
Owen had never seen there before. It took Owen until much later that night
to understand it was pride.

"Well," Pop said, and his voice came out rough. "I'll be damned."

Then he went and got the mop, because some things don't change just because
your son can float a milk bottle.

---

Mom came home from the cannery at six, smelling like brine and tin the way
she always did. She'd barely gotten through the door before Tess was hanging
off her arm, shouting the whole day at her in one breath: *he got Gravity,
it's rare, the lady's only stamped two, and he made the milk FLOAT, Mom, in
the AIR, and Mrs. Delgado's cousin says Talia was THERE, at the Bindery, and
Owen walked right past her.*

The kitchen went quiet.

Mom looked at Owen, who was sitting at the table with the book in front of
him, very interested in the tablecloth.

"Is that right?" she said.

Owen didn't answer.

Mom looked at him a moment longer. Then she set her bag down on the hook
and said, "Let's see it, then," and he slid the book across, grateful.

She didn't open it. She ran her fingers over the mark on the cover, slowly,
the way she checked a tin can for dents on the line. She turned it over and
looked at the back. She weighed it in her hands.

"It's heavy," she said.

"It's thick. Mrs. Harrow said the pages fill slowly. For rare ones."

"Mm." Mom set it down. Then she came around the table and put both hands on
the sides of his face, and looked at him the way she used to when he was
little and had a fever, like she was checking him for something. "Happy
birthday, sweetheart," she said, and kissed his forehead.

It was a good night after that, mostly. Mrs. Abernathy came up from
downstairs because she'd heard the noise, and brought a jar of plums. Then
the Delgados came up from the first floor, and the family from across the
landing, and then people Owen had only ever seen on the stairs, until the
kitchen was packed wall to wall and the windows had steamed over. Pop went
down to the corner and came back with a cake from Sorrell's bakery, the kind
with real sugar icing, which they could not afford and which nobody
mentioned. It said HAPPY BIRTHDAY OWEN across the top in blue, and under it,
in smaller letters that Tess had clearly bullied the baker into adding,
GRAVITY!!

Everybody wanted to see the mark. Nobody asked him to do Lighten again,
because his head still hurt, so Tess did it for him instead. She acted out
the milk bottle for every new person who came through the door, with sound
effects, getting bigger and more dramatic every time, until by the fifth
telling the bottle had been falling from the *ceiling*.

At half past eight there was a knock, and a boy in a Ministry messenger's
uniform stood on the landing holding a small white box tied with gold
string. The whole kitchen went quiet. Owen took it, because everyone was
looking at him, and opened it, because everyone was still looking at him.

Inside was a single ticket for Saturday at Harbor Arena, front row, and a
note in the same handwriting as Tess's card.

*Happy birthday, Owen.*

*I deserved that today. I deserved worse. I know what I did, that day at the
Bindery. I've known every single day since.*

*There's a reason. It isn't a good enough one, and I'm not allowed to put it
in a letter. But I'll tell you, if you'll let me. Come Saturday? You don't
have to talk to me. Just come.*

*—T.*

*P.S. I know you drew the mustache.*

Owen read it twice standing up. Then he folded it very small and put it in
his pocket, and when Mrs. Abernathy asked what it said, he said, "Just happy
birthday," and went back to his cake.

---

Later, when the kitchen got too hot and too loud, Owen slipped out onto the
landing and sat on the top step. Through the door behind him he could hear
Mrs. Abernathy telling a story, and Pop laughing at it, and somebody's radio
playing dance music.

After a minute, the door opened and closed again, and Tess sat down next to
him in her nightgown with her cards in her lap. She didn't say anything for a
while, which for Tess was a kind of miracle.

"Did you really walk past her?" she asked finally.

"Yeah."

"Like, *right* past her?"

"Right past her." He stared down the dark stairwell. "Close enough to touch."

Tess shuffled her cards. She pulled out the shiny one from the middle, the
one with the blue ink on it, and looked at it.

"That's what she did to you," she said. "At her Bindery day. Isn't it."

Owen turned and looked at her.

"Pop told Mom," Tess said. "I was supposed to be asleep. I'm never actually
asleep." She ran her thumb over the gold letters on the card. "Was it bad?
When she did it?"

It was such a simple question. Owen had spent five months not answering it,
not even to himself, and his ten-year-old sister had just put it down on the
step between them like a card.

"Yeah," he said. His voice came out strange. "It was bad. She was my best
friend, Tess. She was my best friend since we were *seven*, and she looked
at me like she didn't know who I was. And then she just... left. And she
never wrote. Not once. She sent *you* a card." He heard how that sounded and
winced. "I'm not mad at you. I'm glad you got it. I just..."

"You wanted her to know what it felt like," Tess said.

"Yeah."

"Did it feel good?"

Owen thought about pushing out through the bronze doors into the sunlight.
The way it had stopped feeling good before he'd even reached the steps.

"No," he said.

Tess nodded, very seriously, the way she did when she was thinking about a
trade.

"She says *Cable Street* on the radio," she said. "Every time. Last night,
after the exhibition, the man asked her who it was for, and she said *Cable
Street.* You turned it off this morning before it got to that part." She
held out the card to him. "I don't think you say *Cable Street* on the radio
if you don't know who somebody is."

Owen looked at the card. At Talia's calm, serious face, and the light
pouring off her fingers, and the smudged blue ink underneath. *Don't trade
me.*

He felt the folded note in his pocket. *There's a reason.*

"You can still be mad and go Saturday," Tess said. "I'm mad at you *all the
time*, and I still let you have my plums."

"You've never once let me have your plums."

"I *would*," said Tess, with dignity, and leaned her head on his shoulder.

They sat there a while longer, listening to the party through the door. Down
in the street, a tram went by with its wire sparking blue. By the time the
song on the radio ended, Tess was asleep against his arm, still holding the
card.

---

Later, when the neighbors had gone home and Pop had carried Tess to bed,
Owen came into the kitchen for a glass of water and found his parents at the
table, talking low. They stopped when he came in.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing," said Pop.

Mom looked at Pop, and then at Owen. She'd always been the one who told him
the truth when Pop wanted to spare him from it.

"You watch what's happened to Talia," she said. "That girl hasn't slept
right in five months. You can see it on her face in the newspaper. Rare
things get noticed, Owen. People are going to want things from you now. Some
of them will be nice about it and some of them won't. You understand?"

He thought about the shadows under Talia's eyes. About Mr. Crane saying
*people are watching.* About the note in his pocket. *I'm not allowed to put
it in a letter.*

"It's not like that," he said. "It's not Grandmaster. It's just rare."

"I know it's not." Mom squeezed his hand once and let go. "I'm just telling
you." She paused. "And Owen? Whatever it is between you and that girl. Don't
leave it too long. Things you leave too long get heavy."

He looked at her. She looked back, and he understood that she'd seen all of
it, the whole five months, and hadn't said a word until now.

"Go to bed," Mom said. "You've got school in the morning. Gravity or no
gravity."

---

Owen couldn't sleep.

He climbed out his window and sat on the roof of the tenement with his back
against the chimney and the book in his lap. It was the spot where he and
Talia used to sit when they were kids, eating stolen plums and making up
stories about what the rich people did in the towers on the hill. Now one of
the rich people in the towers on the hill was her.

Below him, Port Aldren glittered all the way up the hill: the harbor lights,
the neon, and at the very top, the Ministry towers, where one of the high
windows was still lit.

He wondered if it was hers. He'd never know. She'd never told him.

He took the note out of his pocket and unfolded it and read it again under
the streetlight. *There's a reason. It isn't a good enough one.*

He didn't know what to do with that. He'd spent five months building a wall
out of being angry, one brick at a time, and today he'd finally gotten to
use it. And it turned out that walking past her hadn't made him feel any
less like the kid left standing with his hand in the air. It had just made
two of them.

He folded the note back up and put it away, and turned the empty pages of his
book instead, one after another, trying to imagine what would be on them.
Lifting a tram. Catching someone falling off a crane. Being somebody nobody
could look straight through ever again.

The thing he kept coming back to, though, wasn't the milk bottle or the
rope, or even Talia. It was that half second at the desk, before the weight.
The heat and the cold and the floor tipping. Everything at once. He'd
watched a dozen kids touch their books that morning, and none of them had
looked like they'd felt anything like that. They'd just gotten warm, or wet,
or strong.

Owen had decided not to mention it to anyone. It had only been half a
second. It was probably nerves.

He closed the book and ran his thumb over the mark on the cover.

For a second, under the streetlight, it looked a little wrong. The lines
seemed deeper than they should have been, as if the mark had been drawn very
carefully over the top of a different one, and the one underneath was
bigger.

He blinked.

It was just the Gravity mark again. Just ink on grey cloth.

Owen looked up at the lit window at the top of the hill for a long time.

*Saturday,* he thought. He still didn't know if he was going.

He yawned, tucked the book under his arm, and climbed back in through the
window to bed.

---

Across town, in a small apartment above a shoe repair shop, Mrs. Harrow sat
at her kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold beside her.

In front of her was a tin box that had belonged to her grandmother. She
hadn't opened it in years. Inside, under a bundle of old letters and a pair
of spectacles with one lens cracked, was a single folded page, soft as cloth
from being folded and unfolded so many times.

Her grandmother had worked at the Bindery too, a long time ago. When Odile
was a girl, her grandmother used to sit her on her knee and tell her stories
that nobody else's grandmother told. She said there had once been fourteen
marks on the Bindery ceiling, not seven. She said people had simply stopped
talking about the other seven, and then stopped teaching them, and then,
after long enough, stopped remembering them at all, the way a town forgets
the name of a street after the sign falls down.

Odile had thought they were fairy stories. Mostly, she still did.

She unfolded the page and laid it flat under the lamp.

On it, in her grandmother's careful hand, were six marks drawn in a row in
faded ink, each with a word written underneath. Gravity was one of them. It
looked exactly like the one she'd stamped into the ledger that morning.

But that wasn't the one she was looking at.

At the very bottom of the page, set apart from the others, there was one
more mark, larger than the rest. Her grandmother had drawn it with more care
than all the others put together. And inside it, if you knew where to look,
fitted neatly among its lines as if it had always been just one small piece
of something bigger, was the Gravity mark.

Under it, her grandmother had written a single word:

*Reqrium.*

And under that, in smaller letters: *All of them.*

Mrs. Harrow sat and looked at it for a long time. She thought about the
Reyes girl five months ago, and the whole hall going white. She thought
about that same girl this morning, standing silent behind a boy who wouldn't
turn around, while every eye in the building watched her and not one of them
watched him. She thought about the pencil on Mr. Pettibone's desk, rolling
the wrong way for one breath. She thought about the boy's face when she'd
asked him what he'd felt, and the half second he'd waited before he said
*cold.*

Then she folded the page back up along its soft old creases, put it back in
the tin box under the letters, and closed the lid.

It was probably nothing. It was probably just a rare book and an old woman's
memory of fairy stories.

But tomorrow, she decided, she would pull the file for number
four-four-one-nine and find out where Owen Calder lived.

Just to keep an eye on him.

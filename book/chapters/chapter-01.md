# Chapter One — A Cold Guest

The kettle came to a boil the way it always did, with a small complaint and
then a long even sigh, and Wren lifted it off the hook before it could work
itself into a whistle. There was no one left to serve. It was habit that had
set the water on — the same habit that had banked the fire, wiped the long
table, turned the three good cups mouth-down on the shelf so they wouldn't
gather cold in the night. You kept the motions whether or not the town gave
you a reason to. The motions were how you stayed a place worth stopping at.

Outside, the Road was black and the wind had teeth. She could hear it
testing the shutters, patient as a creditor. Somewhere out past the last of
Lowfen's shuttered houses the Grey sat in the dark where it always sat, a
long day's walk to the north and closer every year, and on a night like this
you could almost feel it out there the way you feel a cold window across a
room without touching it.

Wren poured herself the tea she hadn't meant to make and stood with her back
to the hearth, letting the warmth press into her through her coat, and did
not think about the empty chair at the north end of the table. She had
gotten very good, over the years, at the precise labor of not thinking about
the empty chair. It was a skill like any other. You practiced it until your
hands knew it.

The fire was good tonight. She checked it the way she checked everything,
without looking — reaching out with the old sense she tried not to use for
anything more than housekeeping, feeling the shape of the warmth in the
room. Strong at the hearth. Thinning at the door, where the wind picked at
the gap under the threshold. A soft banked pocket in the corner where the
cat would sleep if the cat ever came back. Honest heat, all of it. Fire and
bodies and the slow warmth a good building hoards through a day. Nothing
taken from anything that hadn't given it. She had made herself a life you
could measure that way, and most nights the measuring was enough.

She was three swallows into the tea when something knocked at the door.

Not the wind. The wind didn't knock; the wind leaned. This was three raps,
low down on the wood, the height a knuckle finds if the person is small or
the person is kneeling. Wren set the cup on the table without a sound and
stood still, listening, the way you learn to stand still in a war and never
quite unlearn.

The raps came again. Weaker.

"Kettle's cold," she called, which was a lie, and the lie was the closing
that everyone on the Emberline understood: *no room, no fire, move on to the
next warm door.* Except there was no next warm door. Lowfen was the last
bead on the wire out here; north of the Kettle-and-Coal the Road ran out
into dark and frost and the swallowed country, and everyone who traveled the
Warm Road knew it, which meant no one knocked at Wren's door going north.
They only ever knocked coming back.

Or they didn't come from the Road at all.

She crossed the room. She was aware, distantly, of the poker by the hearth,
and more aware of the fact that in twenty years she had never once needed
it. She lifted the bar and opened the door against the wind.

A child stood on the step.

Wren's first thought, before anything else, before fear or pity, was a
craftsman's thought, the thought her old life had ground into her so deep it
came before her own name: *she's cold wrong.* The girl was small, seven or
eight or a starved ten, wrapped in a coat that had been good once and
several sizes too big for whoever it had been good for. Frost had crusted
her lashes and the wool at her collar. Her lips had gone the color that
lips are not supposed to go. She had walked out of the north, out of the
dark where nothing walked, and by every rule Wren knew she should have been
a body in the snow a mile back.

She was not a body. She was standing. And when Wren reached out with the old
sense, without meaning to, purely from reflex — the same reflex that read
the fire — she found that the child was cold, deeply cold, dying-cold at the
edges, and that clutched against her chest, under the too-big coat, wrapped
in what looked like a strip of blanket, there was a *warmth.*

A warmth that should not have been there.

Wren had spent a lifetime learning the shape of heat. She could tell a
banked hearth from a fresh one blind. She could tell the flat, honest warmth
of a fire from the sweeter, faster warmth of a living body, and she could
tell both of those from the third kind, the kind she did not let herself
remember on most nights, the stolen kind, that burned hot and wrong and left
a cold place where it had been torn from. She knew all the warmths there
were.

She did not know this one.

It was not fire. It was not the child's own body — the child's body was
losing its heat, was down to the last coals of itself. It was not stolen;
there was no cold wound behind it, no husk it had been reaped from. It sat in
the bundle against the girl's chest and it *gave,* steady and full and
sourceless, warm as a summer that this world had not had in a thousand
years, and it did not cool, and Wren stood in her own doorway with the wind
howling past her and felt, for the first time in longer than she could name,
afraid.

The child looked up at her. Her eyes were the only fast thing about her.

"Cold's not," she said, through lips that barely worked. It took Wren a
moment to understand it was an answer. *Kettle's cold,* Wren had said. *Cold's
not,* the girl answered, and there was something in it that was almost a joke
and almost an accusation, this small frozen creature calling out the lie of
a grown woman who had the best fire for forty miles going at her back.

Wren stepped aside and held the door.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Come in before you let all the warm out."

The girl came in. She did not stumble, which frightened Wren more than
stumbling would have; she walked to the fire with the flat, careful economy
of someone spending the very last of what she had and knowing exactly how
little was left. Wren barred the door behind her and the room closed warm
around them both, and outside the wind found the door shut against it and
leaned, and leaned, and gave up nothing.

Wren got the girl into the chair nearest the hearth — not the empty one at
the north end; she found she could not have put a living child in that one to
save her life, and did not examine why. She got the big coat off her, and it
was heavier than it should have been, sodden through, and under it the girl
was all sticks and hollows. She got the good blanket off the shelf and the
warming-pan she kept for the letting-rooms, and she banked the fire up higher
than she'd allow on any ordinary night, and through all of it the girl kept
both hands over the bundle at her chest and would not, would *not,* let it be
taken, not for the blanket, not for the coat, not for anything.

"All right," Wren said. "All right. Nobody's taking it. Keep it. Just let me
see your hands, they're worse than the rest of you."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then, one hand at
a time, keeping the other pinned over the bundle, she let Wren chafe the
feeling back into her fingers, and Wren felt the small warmth coming up under
her own hands, the ordinary miracle of a body deciding to keep living, and it
steadied her more than she wanted to admit. This, at least, she knew how to
do. Warm a cold child. Anyone could do it. It was the oldest and simplest
kindness there was, and the whole of the Emberline's law and grief and war
came down, in the end, to arguments about that one small act — who owed it,
who was allowed it, and what a person was permitted to burn to give it.

She put the kettle back on. She was not going to think about the bundle yet.
She was going to make the child tea, because that was the motion, and the
motion was how you stayed a person worth being cold and lost and eight years
old in front of.

"There's honey," she said, over her shoulder. "For the tea. If you've got a
sweet tooth. Some do."

The girl said nothing. When Wren turned back with the cup, the girl was
watching her with those quick eyes, and she had shifted the blanket just
enough that the edge of the bundle showed at her chest, the strip of frayed
wool, and in the gap where it had come loose Wren could see, for one instant
before the child drew the blanket closed again, a dull red glow.

A coal. An ordinary-looking coal, small enough to hide in two child's hands.

Glowing steady in a wet blanket, in a cold room an hour ago, on a road out
of the country where nothing living walked. Glowing with no fire under it and
no fuel around it and no cold wound behind it. Giving out that warmth she did
not have a name for, the sourceless summer warmth, the thing the rule of the
whole world said could not exist — *you cannot make more warmth than the
living world provides; every warm room is warm because something is colder*
— giving it out and out and out and not going dim.

Wren stood very still with the cup of tea in her hands.

She thought about the war. She thought about the reaping years, and the
things she had done to hold a line, and the reasons that had seemed like
enough at the time, and the smallest town on the Emberline that would still
have her afterward, and twenty years of honest fire and turned-down cups and
the careful daily labor of being a place worth stopping at and nothing more.
She thought about how a coal that never cooled was either the salvation of a
dying world or the worst thing that had ever come down the Warm Road, and
about how she, of all the people it could have walked out of the dark
toward, had the misfortune of being one of the few still living who might
know the difference — and might, God help her, be wrong about it again.

The girl drank her tea. Her color was coming back. Outside, the wind leaned
on the door, patient as the Grey, patient as everything cold, and got
nothing.

"What's your name?" Wren asked.

The child looked at her over the rim of the cup, deciding whether the
question was safe.

"Pell," she said at last. And then, because a child that cold is also a
child, and because the tea was hot and sweet and the room was the warmest
she had been in longer than Wren wanted to imagine: "Is there more honey?"

"There's more honey," Wren said.

She got the honey. Her hands, she noticed, were steady. That was the old
skill too — steady hands over a fear you couldn't afford to show. She had
hoped never to need it again. She set the honey down in front of the child
who had carried the impossible out of the dead country in her two small
hands, and she pulled up a chair, not the empty one, and she sat, and the
fire burned honest at her back for one more hour while it still could.

Then she said, gently, the way you say a thing you already know the shape of
and dread:

"Pell. That coal you're carrying. I need you to tell me where you got it."

The wind stopped, just then, the way it does — a held breath between one gust
and the next — and in the sudden quiet the girl's face did something that
was much too old for it, and Wren understood, before the child said a single
word, that the warm road behind her had run out a long, long way back.

# Chapter Three: The Aldren Institute

They took the Binders off in the morning.

It was the old warden who did it, the one with the folded-map face. He came in with a ring of small iron keys and knelt behind Owen on the cot without a word, and there was a series of heavy clicks, and then a sound like a held breath let out, and the cold went away.

It went away all at once. One moment it was there, packed into Owen's arms and chest like frozen mud; the next it was simply gone, and in the space it left behind, something rushed back in. Owen gasped. It was like surfacing from the bottom of the harbor. His whole body was suddenly too heavy and too light at the same time, and the air in the cell tilted, very slightly, toward him, the way water tilts in a glass when you lean it.

The bulb over the door swung on its wire.

"Easy," said the warden, very quietly. He had one hand flat on Owen's shoulder blade. "Easy. Breathe. Let it settle."

Owen breathed. The bulb slowed. The air straightened itself out. After a while he found he could bring his hands around in front of him, and he sat there looking at them. There were red rings worn into both wrists, raw at the edges. His fingers shook.

"Thank you," he said.

The warden grunted. He stood up, slowly, one knee cracking, and hung the Binders on a hook on his belt, where they clinked against each other like a bad-luck charm.

"What's your name?" Owen asked him. "You never said."

The warden looked at him for a moment. Then he looked away.

"Doesn't matter," he said. "Won't be seeing much of each other after today."

"Why not?"

But Ansel Vey was in the doorway, and the warden stepped aside without another word.

---

Vey had an apple again. He also had a paper parcel tied with string, which he tossed onto the cot next to Owen.

"Clothes," he said. "You can't go up the hill smelling like cell four. Wash first. There's a basin across the corridor." He bit into the apple. "Then we'll have a little talk, and then you'll have a little drive, and then you'll have a whole new life, and I'll finally have my breakfast. It's a big day for both of us."

Owen looked at the parcel. He didn't touch it.

"Up the hill," he said.

"Up the hill." Vey smiled. "You've been wanting to go up that hill for five months, haven't you? Well. Here's your chance."

Owen's face must have done something, because Vey laughed.

"It's in your file," he said. "Everything's in your file." He pulled the wooden chair in from the corridor with his foot and sat on it backward, arms folded across the top, apple in one hand. "Now listen, because this part matters, and I'm only going to say it once.

"You're going to the Aldren Institute. You'll have heard of it."

Owen had. Everyone had. The Aldren Institute sat on the crown of the hill, right beside the Ministry towers, behind a wall so high you couldn't see anything over it but the tops of its spires. It was where the country sent the best of the best: the strongest fire, the fastest air, the kids who could lift a freighter or boil a lake. And the Grandmasters. Every new Grandmaster went to the Institute to learn how to carry what they'd been given. Every trading card in Tess's collection had *Aldren Institute* printed in tiny letters under the name.

It was where Talia had gone.

"You're our Gravity scholarship," said Vey. "A rare-power boy from the docks, found at the Bindery, sponsored by a generous and anonymous branch of the Ministry. That's what your file says upstairs. That's what the other students will be told, and it's what you'll tell them. You'll have lessons. You'll have a bed with a real mattress and three hot meals a day and all the training anyone in this country can give. The headmistress knows what you really are. Nobody else does. Keep it that way."

"And if I don't?"

"Ah." Vey finished the apple and dropped the core into the bucket, the way he had the day before. "That's the other thing.

"Last night, while you were asleep, we gave you a little something." He said it lightly, as if he were talking about a vitamin. "Standard for the Low Office. You didn't feel it. You won't. It won't hurt you, and it won't get in your way, and you'll forget it's there. But if you run, or if you tell a living soul what you really carry, or if you hurt somebody we'd prefer you didn't hurt, then one night you'll go to sleep in your nice new bed and you simply won't wake up."

He tapped the side of his own neck, lightly, with two fingers, and smiled.

Owen's hand went to his own neck before he could stop it. There was nothing there. No mark. No scar. Just skin.

"I didn't feel anything," he said.

"You weren't meant to."

"When? When did you...?"

"While you slept," said Vey patiently. "I did say." He stood up and swung the chair back into the corridor with one hand. "Wash. Dress. The car's waiting. Welcome to the rest of your life, Owen."

---

The lift went up for a very long time.

Nobody put the Binders back on him. Two wardens rode with him, one on each side, and neither of them looked at him, and the iron box with his book in it sat on the floor of the cage between his feet. He could feel it through the iron. It was humming, faintly, like a kettle a long way off.

Then the cage stopped, and the gate opened, and there was the tunnel, and the motorcar, and the long curve of old brick going up. At the top the iron gates swung open by themselves, and the car rolled up the ramp beside the Bindery and out into the street.

Into the light.

Owen hadn't known he'd missed it. He'd only been underground a night and a day. But when the sun hit the windscreen he had to close his eyes, and when he opened them again the whole city was there, bright and loud and ordinary, and it was the strangest thing he had ever seen. Trams sparking on their wires. A newsboy yelling on the corner. A woman watering geraniums with one lazy finger, the way Mrs. Abernathy did. Nobody looking twice at a black motorcar going by.

The Bindery was closed. There were wooden barriers across the bronze doors and a painted sign propped against them: CLOSED FOR REPAIRS. GAS LEAK. REGISTRATION AT THE WREN STREET ANNEX. A policeman was standing beside it, directing a small crowd of sixteen-year-olds and their mothers around the corner.

Owen watched them go. He wondered if any of them had been in the hall yesterday. If any of them were telling their friends about the boy who'd crushed everyone flat.

Then the car turned onto Crown Avenue, and started to climb.

He'd walked partway up this hill a hundred times, but never to the top. The buildings got taller, and cleaner, and quieter. The shop signs changed from hand-painted wood to gold letters on glass. The motorcars at the curbs got longer and shinier. The people on the pavement wore hats that cost more than Pop made in a month. Halfway up, the tram lines ended, as if the trams weren't allowed any farther.

At the very top of the hill, the Ministry towers rose white against the sky, with the silver airship moored at the mast. And beside them, behind a high grey wall topped with iron spikes, stood the Aldren Institute.

It was bigger than Owen had imagined. It was old, much older than the towers, built of dark stone gone green in places, with spires and slate roofs and a great clock tower in the middle. The gates were black iron worked into the shapes of the seven Grandmaster marks, flame and water and air and earth and light and ice and thunder, all twisted together. The car rolled through them, and they closed behind it with a clang that Owen felt in his teeth.

And then he saw the students.

There were hundreds of them. In the courtyard, on the steps, leaning out of windows. They were his age and a little older, all in charcoal-grey coats with a thin stripe of colour at the cuffs: red for fire, blue for water, green for earth, and a dozen other colours he didn't know yet. Some of them were throwing a ball of water back and forth across the courtyard without touching it. Two boys were having a lazy argument with small flames flickering over their knuckles. A girl sat on the edge of a fountain, reading, while the pages turned by themselves in a breeze only her book could feel.

They all stopped when the car pulled up.

Owen saw it happen. He saw one head turn, then another, then a whole group by the fountain. He saw a boy lean over to whisper to a girl, and the girl's eyes go wide. He saw someone point.

He heard it, too, before he'd even opened the door. It came across the courtyard like wind across the harbor:

*That's him.*

*The Bindery.*

*My cousin was there. She said the whole floor cracked.*

*She said he crushed a man's skull.*

*That's not true. It was a gas leak. It was in the papers.*

*Then why does he have wardens with him?*

The warden on Owen's left opened the car door. Owen got out, holding the iron box against his chest, because nobody had told him not to, and stood on the gravel in his new clothes and his old boots while three hundred of the most powerful young people in the country stared at him.

He'd wanted to be seen. That was what he'd wished for, over the candle in the bun. *Something nobody could look straight through.*

Well. Nobody was looking through him now.

---

The headmistress's office was at the top of the clock tower, up a stone staircase so narrow and twisting that the wardens had to go single file. The door at the top was oak, and it opened before anyone knocked.

"Leave him," said a voice from inside. "And leave the box."

The wardens set the box down at Owen's feet, and went back down the stairs, and Owen was alone in the doorway.

The office was round, and full of books, and full of rocks. There were rocks on every surface: on the desk, on the shelves between the books, on the windowsills, in glass cases along the walls. Rough ones and polished ones, grey and red and green and one that was so black it seemed to drink the light. A huge clock face took up one whole wall, the back of the tower clock, its gears turning slowly behind the glass.

In front of it, in a heavy wooden chair, sat a very old woman.

She was small and upright, with iron-grey hair cut short as a man's and a face like a cliff: all hard edges, weathered in. She wore the white coat of a Grandmaster, but hers was old and plain and patched at one elbow, and the mark stitched on her shoulder was green. Owen knew that mark. It was on the second card from the left in Tess's collection.

The Earth Grandmaster.

"Ida Kessler," she said. "Headmistress of this Institute for thirty-one years, and Grandmaster of Earth for forty-four." She looked at him for a long time, the way you'd look at a crack in a wall to see if it was going to spread. "You're shorter than I expected."

Owen didn't know what to say to that. So he said nothing.

"Sit."

He sat, in a hard chair across the desk from her. The iron box he set on his knees.

"Put that on the floor," said Kessler. "You don't need to hold it like a baby. It's not going anywhere without you. It *can't.*"

He put it on the floor.

"Ansel Vey tells me you're clever," Kessler went on, "and stubborn, and angry, and that you said no to the thing two doors down, which I will admit is more than most." Her eyes narrowed very slightly. "He also tells me you're the most dangerous child ever to walk through my gates, and that I'm to teach you to be more dangerous still. Do you know how many holders of the dark books I've met, Mr. Calder?"

"No, ma'am."

"Four," said Kessler. "In forty-four years. Every one of them was dead within the year." She said it flatly, without cruelty, the way she might have said it would rain. "I'm telling you that so you understand what I think the odds are. Not so you'll feel sorry for yourself. I don't have time for that and neither do you."

Owen's hands had curled into fists on his knees. He made them open.

"Here is how this will go," said Kessler. "In the mornings, you'll attend lessons with the other first-years. History, theory, discipline, the same as everyone. In the afternoons, you'll train with me, alone, in the Deep Room under this tower, where you can't bring anything down on anyone's head. At night, you'll sleep in the east dormitory with the other boys, because a boy who is locked away from everyone becomes a monster faster than a boy who has to share a washroom. Any questions?"

"Why are you helping them?" Owen said.

He hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway. Kessler's eyebrows went up a fraction of an inch.

"The Low Office," Owen said. "They kill kids. In a basement. They showed me the form. And you're a Grandmaster. You're supposed to be..." He didn't know how to finish it. *Good,* he wanted to say. *You're on a trading card. You're supposed to be good.*

Kessler was quiet for a moment. Behind her, the great clock gears ground slowly around.

"I've seen the Waste," she said at last. "On the eastern coast. I went there when I was about your age, on a dare. Two hundred miles where nothing grows. Where the stones are melted into glass and the glass is still warm, a hundred and twenty years later." She looked at him steadily. "I'm not helping them, Mr. Calder. I'm helping whatever's left of this country after you six are finished with it. If that means helping them, then I'll help them. If it means helping you, I'll help you. If it means stopping you," she said, "I'll do that too."

She let that sit.

"Now get out. Your things are in the east dormitory, room nine. Trial's at noon. Don't be late. I hate late."

---

Room nine was at the end of a long, creaking corridor on the top floor of the east dormitory. It had two beds, two desks, two wardrobes, and one tall window looking out over the whole city, all the way down the hill to the harbor, so that Owen could see the cranes and the cannery smokestacks and, if he squinted, the grey smudge where Cable Street should be.

It also had a boy in it.

He was sitting on the bed by the window with his boots on the blanket, reading a newspaper. He was about Owen's height, with neat dark hair and a face that looked like it had never once been out in the rain. His coat had a red stripe at the cuff, and it fit him the way coats only fit people who'd had them made.

He didn't look up when Owen came in.

"You're on the wrong floor," he said. "The scholarship rooms are in the basement."

"This is room nine," said Owen. "They told me room nine."

The boy lowered the newspaper. He looked at Owen, at his old boots, at the iron box under his arm, and something went across his face that Owen recognized immediately, because he'd seen it on a hundred faces up and down Crown Avenue. It was the look you gave a stain.

"Oh," the boy said. "Oh, you're *him.*"

He folded the newspaper very carefully and set it down.

"Julian Vance." He didn't hold out his hand. "My father sits on the Ministry council. My godfather is Cassius Rourke. You'll have heard of him, even on the docks." He tilted his head. "I asked for a single room. They told me there weren't any left. And then this morning they told me I'd be sharing with the *Gravity scholarship.*" He said the words like they tasted bad. "So I'd like to know, Calder, before I go and complain to my father, what exactly you did to get a room on the top floor of the east dormitory, when your father shovels coal?"

Owen set the iron box down on the empty bed.

"How do you know what my father does?"

"Everyone knows." Julian picked up the newspaper again and held it up so Owen could see the page. There was a small paragraph near the bottom: BINDERY CLOSED AFTER GAS LEAK. NO SERIOUS INJURIES. And under it, smaller: *One boy, 16, of Cable Street, briefly unaccounted for, has since been found safe and awarded a Ministry scholarship.* "It's a very small town, once you get to the top of the hill. And people *talk.*"

He looked at Owen over the top of the paper.

"My cousin was in that hall, you know," he said softly. "She says it wasn't a gas leak. She says you put her face into the floor so hard she broke a tooth."

Owen didn't say anything.

"She says you *liked* it," said Julian.

The window glass rattled very slightly in its frame.

Julian's eyes flicked to it. Then back to Owen. For just a second, something moved in his face that wasn't contempt.

Owen turned around, and put his things away in the empty wardrobe, and didn't say another word.

---

The noon bell was ringing when he came down into the courtyard, and the students were streaming toward the great hall on the north side, and that was where Owen saw Jonah Pike.

He saw the motorcar first. It was long and black, with a Ministry flag on the bonnet, and it had pulled right up to the steps of the great hall with a small crowd of students around it, and two men with flash cameras. As Owen watched, the back door opened, and someone climbed out, blinking in the sunlight, and the cameras went off *pop pop pop*, and the crowd cheered.

He was tall. Taller than Owen remembered. All long arms and long legs, with ash-brown hair sticking up in every direction like he'd just stuck his finger in a light socket. He was wearing a brand-new storm-grey coat with a gold mark stitched on the chest, and he looked completely terrified.

Jonah.

For a second Owen couldn't move.

They'd been born the same night, in the same hospital ward, twenty minutes apart, on either side of midnight. They'd had every birthday party together until they were ten. They'd learned to swim together off the end of Pier Six, and gotten caught stealing plums from the same barrow, and sat on the same roof on the same summer nights, him and Jonah and Talia, the three of them, eating those plums and spitting the stones at the gulls.

And then Talia had left. And Owen had stopped going up on the roof. He'd stopped going anywhere much. He'd stopped answering when Jonah knocked, and after a while Jonah had stopped knocking, and they'd become two people who nodded to each other on Cable Street and didn't say much else. Owen didn't even know when it had happened. It had just happened, the way the tide goes out.

Twenty minutes after midnight. Which meant that on paper, Jonah's birthday had been the day after Owen's. Which meant Jonah had gone to the Bindery yesterday, to the annex on Wren Street, with the main hall shut up for the gas leak.

And the Thunder book had been waiting.

*Lightning doesn't strike the same street twice,* Pop had said.

Jonah was looking around the courtyard with his mouth half open, like someone who'd woken up on the wrong tram. His eyes went over the crowd, and the spires, and the fountain, and then they landed on Owen, and stopped.

Owen watched his face change. Surprise, first. Then something that might have been relief. Then something more careful.

Jonah said something to the man with the camera, and pushed through the crowd, and came across the gravel toward him, fast, with his long legs, until he was standing right in front of Owen. Up close he smelled faintly of ozone, like the air before a storm.

"Owen," he said.

"Hey, Jonah."

"You're..." Jonah stopped. Started again. "They said you were missing. After the Bindery. Your ma was going door to door. Mine went with her. Then yesterday morning a man in a hat came and said you'd got a *scholarship*..." He was looking at Owen very hard. "And now you're here."

"I'm here."

"At the *Institute.*"

"Yeah."

"With *Gravity.*"

"Yeah."

Jonah looked at him for a long moment, and Owen could see him thinking, the way he did about once a week, when it mattered. Then he let out a breath and pushed a hand through his hair, which made it stick up worse.

"I got Thunder," he said. "Yesterday. At the annex. The ceiling of the little room lit up and I thought it was a fire and I tried to get out through a *window*." He laughed, but it came out shaky. "They kept me up the hill all night signing things, and this morning they put me in a car with the Prime Minister's flag on it, Owen. I've been sick twice."

Owen didn't know what to say. He found, to his surprise, that he wanted to laugh.

"You were always going to be sick twice," he said.

Jonah grinned. It was the same grin he'd had when they were seven. Then it faded, and he looked at Owen more carefully.

"You look like you haven't slept," he said. "Like, for a week. What happened, Owen? At the Bindery. For real. My ma heard the gas story, but Mrs. Delgado's cousin was *there,* and she says..."

"It was a gas leak," said Owen.

Jonah looked at him.

"It was a gas leak," Owen said again, "and I got lucky, and I got a scholarship, and here I am." He could feel the lie sitting in his mouth like a stone. He thought about Vey tapping two fingers against his neck. *If you tell a living soul.* "That's all."

"Right," said Jonah slowly. "Right. Okay." He was quiet for a second. "Talia came down to Cable Street last night, you know. First time in five months. She went to your door. I saw her from my window. She was talking to your ma on the landing for ages."

Something cold went through Owen's chest.

"She was probably told to," he said.

Jonah's eyebrows went up. "Told to? By who?"

But the noon bell rang again, louder, and a teacher in a black gown was on the steps of the great hall, calling out, "First-years! Trial! *Now,* please!" And the crowd was moving, and Jonah was being swept up in it by two men in grey suits who seemed to have appeared out of nowhere, and Owen found himself carried along through the great doors on his own.

---

The great hall of the Aldren Institute was built for this.

It was long and high, with a floor of bare stone and galleries running around the walls, crowded now with older students leaning over the rails to watch. At the far end, on a raised stone platform, sat the teachers in their black gowns. In the middle of them, in her patched white coat, sat Ida Kessler. And beside Kessler, in a long white coat with a gold sunburst stitched on the shoulder, her hair tied back in a high ponytail with a gold ribbon, sat Talia.

She was looking right at him.

Owen looked away.

In the middle of the hall floor, on a thick iron plate, sat a block of dark granite. It was about the size of a small motorcar. It had a groove worn around its middle, where a century of chains had been wrapped around it and taken off again.

"The Weighing Stone," said the teacher in the black gown, a thin man with a voice like a creaking door. "Two tons of Harbor granite. Every first-year who has passed through these doors for a hundred years has stood before it and done *something* to it. Lift it. Burn it. Freeze it. Crack it. Move it. It is not a test you can fail. It is a test of *what you are.* The masters will be watching. Names, please."

It went alphabetically.

A girl named Abbot soaked the stone until water ran off it in sheets. A boy named Aldous turned the top of it to rough sand. A girl named Beckett lifted it an inch off the plate with air, her face going purple with effort, and dropped it again with a *boom* that shook the galleries, to applause.

Then: "Calder."

It went quiet.

Owen walked out onto the stone floor. His boots sounded very loud. He could hear the whispers in the galleries stop, one by one, as people noticed who it was. He could feel three hundred sets of eyes on the back of his neck. He could feel one pair of golden ones on the side of his face, from the platform.

He stopped in front of the Weighing Stone.

His griamore was in his hands. They'd let him take it out of the iron box this morning, for the first time since the Bindery. It was heavier than it had any right to be. When he opened it, the first page was there, dark ink on old paper, the single word at the top: **Gravity**. And under it, in smaller writing he hadn't seen before, as if it had been there all along and he'd just never been close enough to read it:

*Lighten. For a moment, the thing you name weighs nothing.*

*Press. For a moment, the thing you name weighs everything.*

*Pull. For a moment, everything falls toward you.*

He knew, looking at them, that the last two weren't for today. He knew it the way you know not to put your hand on a stove. He could feel the weight of them on the page.

He could also feel the hunger.

It was right there at the bottom of him, the way it had been in cell four. Small and patient and empty. Except it wasn't small now. The book was open in his hands and the Binders were gone, and the hunger was *awake*, and it was looking at the Weighing Stone, and at the iron plate under it, and at the stone floor under that, and at the three hundred people in the galleries all around, and it was thinking, very quietly: *More.*

*Pull,* it suggested. *Just once. Just to see.*

His hands were shaking. He could feel the whole hall start, very gently, to lean.

"Mr. Calder." It was Kessler's voice, from the platform. Not loud. Very steady. "Whenever you're ready."

Owen closed his eyes.

He thought about the rope on the dock at home, drifting up like seaweed. The milk bottle hanging in the air above the kitchen floor. He'd never done either of those things. He'd only daydreamed them, on the tram, a thousand times, wondering what it would be like to have a power like the kids on Tess's cards. But he could see them now as clearly as if he had.

*The thing you name.*

He opened his eyes and looked at the Weighing Stone. Only the stone. Not the plate, not the floor, not the people. *That* stone.

"*Lighten,*" he said.

The Weighing Stone rose.

It didn't jump. It didn't jerk. It lifted off the iron plate as smoothly as a soap bubble lifting off a basin, and it kept going, slowly, turning a little as it went: two feet, five feet, ten, until it was hanging in the air above the middle of the great hall at the height of the galleries, two tons of granite floating like a leaf on a pond.

Nobody breathed.

The older students in the gallery nearest it had flattened themselves back against the wall. One of the teachers on the platform had half risen from his chair. Owen could see the stone's shadow drifting slowly across the floor.

And the hunger at the bottom of him was saying, *Higher. Faster. Through the roof. Let them see. Let them all see what you are.*

He could feel his grip slipping. He could feel the stone wanting to go, the way a kite wants to go when the wind catches it. He could feel the hall starting to tilt again, just slightly, toward him.

He thought of Tess on the landing. He thought of Pop's hand on his shoulder. *Good lad.*

"Down," he whispered. "Down. Slowly."

The stone came down.

It came down slowly, turning back the other way, and settled onto the iron plate with a click so soft he barely heard it. Exactly where it had been. As if it had never moved.

Owen let go.

The pain hit him a second later, a white-hot spike behind his eyes, like the worst headache of his life arriving all at once, and he staggered and caught himself with one hand on the cold granite. The book in his other hand had gone warm. He could feel it through the cover.

For a long moment, the great hall was completely silent.

Then someone in the upper gallery started clapping. Then someone else. Then a lot of people, and then the whole hall was clapping and stamping on the wooden galleries, and somebody was whistling, and the thin teacher was writing furiously in a ledger, and the noise came down on Owen like rain.

He looked up at the platform.

Kessler wasn't clapping. She was watching him, her cliff of a face perfectly still, and she gave him the smallest possible nod.

Beside her, Talia wasn't clapping either. She was looking at him with her hands pressed flat together in her lap, and her face had gone very pale, and Owen couldn't tell what she was thinking at all.

He walked back to the line on legs that didn't feel like his. As he passed the students still waiting their turn, he heard one of them whisper, awed, to another: "*That's* the gas leak?"

---

Jonah went after lunch, being a P, and the moment he put his hand on the Weighing Stone, a bolt of blue-white lightning came down out of nowhere, out of the ceiling, and split the granite straight down the middle with a crack like the end of the world. Every window in the great hall blew out at once. The galleries went absolutely wild. Jonah stood there with his hair on end and his mouth open, holding his book, staring at the two smoking halves of the Weighing Stone, and said, in a very small voice that nevertheless carried to every corner of the silent hall:

"*Sorry.*"

Julian Vance went near the end. He set the whole stone on fire, which was supposed to be impossible, and held it burning for a full minute, the flames going from red to orange to a pure hot white, while the older students whistled and stamped. When he walked back past Owen, he didn't look at him. But his jaw was very tight.

---

The Deep Room was under the clock tower, down another twisting staircase, so deep that Owen's ears popped.

It was round, like Kessler's office, and huge, and completely bare. The walls and floor and ceiling were all the same grey stone, smooth as glass, and scored all over with old marks: long gouges, starbursts of cracks, shallow craters, black scorch marks in the shape of hands. There was a single iron door. There was no furniture at all.

"Every Grandmaster in the last hundred years has learned to hold their power in this room," said Kessler. "And one or two other things besides." She stood in the middle of the floor in her patched white coat, and the stone under her boots seemed, somehow, more solid than the stone anywhere else. "Sit."

Owen looked around. "On the floor?"

"On the floor."

He sat. She stayed standing.

"You lifted the stone," she said. "Well done. It was the most controlled thing I've seen a dark holder do." She paused. "It was also the stupidest."

Owen stared up at her.

"You lifted two tons of granite over three hundred people's heads," said Kessler, "on your second day, with a demon at the bottom of you whispering *higher*. I heard it, Mr. Calder. I heard it in the stone. It was all I could do not to put that rock back on the plate myself." She crouched down in front of him, slowly, so their eyes were level. "Why didn't you just slide it an inch? Nobody would have thought less of you."

Owen opened his mouth. Closed it.

"Because they were all looking," he said at last.

"Yes," said Kessler. "Because they were all looking." She stood up. "*That's* what we're going to work on."

She taught him to breathe.

It sounded stupid. It sounded like something you'd teach a baby. But she made him sit on the stone floor with his book shut on his knees and breathe in for four counts and out for six, over and over, until he wanted to scream, and then she made him open the book, and keep breathing.

"The hunger comes in on the in-breath," she said. "It always does, for your kind. Every gasp is it pulling. So you breathe *out,* Mr. Calder. Longer out than in. Every time. You empty yourself before it can fill you."

"How do you know that?" Owen asked. "About my kind?"

"Because I've done this four times before," said Kessler, "and I've watched four children die, and I made very sure to learn something from each of them."

He breathed. In for four. Out for six. The hunger at the bottom of him turned over and grumbled, and the Deep Room leaned very slightly toward him, and then settled again.

"Good," said Kessler. "Again."

They did it for an hour. Then two. Then she made him lighten a pebble, and keep breathing. Then a stone the size of a loaf of bread. Then she made him hold the stone in the air while she walked slowly around him, talking.

"Tell me about the girl," she said.

The stone wobbled.

"What girl?"

"Don't be tiresome. Miss Reyes. The Light Grandmaster, who sat on my platform this morning looking like she'd swallowed a knife, and whom you would not look at." Kessler kept walking. "Your file says you grew up together."

"My file says a lot of things."

"Keep breathing. Out for six." The stone steadied. "Your file says that she ended your friendship on the night she became a Grandmaster, without explanation, and that you've been angry about it for five months. Your file says the demon two doors down knew just where to press, and pressed there, and the whole cell shook." Kessler stopped walking. She was behind him now. He couldn't see her. "Your file says you think she betrayed you to the Low Office."

"She did," said Owen.

The stone jerked upward a foot.

"Out for six," said Kessler sharply.

"She did," Owen said again, and his voice cracked. "She stood there. She watched them take me. Vey said she's been *useful* to them. That's the word he used, *useful*, and she shut the door on me, I brought her a *cake,* and she..."

The Deep Room tilted.

It wasn't slight this time. The whole floor lurched toward him, and Owen felt the hunger roar up from the bottom of him like water up a well, and the stone in the air shot toward the ceiling, and cracks spidered out through the smooth grey floor from where he was sitting, and there was a sound like the whole tower groaning...

And then the floor *moved.*

It came up around him like a hand. The stone of the Deep Room rose in a smooth grey wave and closed over his knees and his hips and his chest, hard and cold, pinning his arms to his sides, holding him in place like a fist. The book slammed shut in his lap. The loaf-sized stone dropped out of the air and cracked on the floor beside him.

The weight went out of the room.

Owen sat there, encased to the shoulders in solid rock, gasping. Kessler walked around in front of him. She hadn't raised her hands. She hadn't opened a book. She was just standing there, a little breathless, looking down at him.

"That," she said, "is why we don't talk about the girl yet."

She let him sit in the stone for a full minute before she let him go. When the rock melted back down into the floor, it left him shaking and soaked in sweat, and his head was pounding so hard his vision had gone spotty at the edges.

"Tomorrow," said Kessler, "we'll do it again. And the day after that. And the day after that. Until the day comes when somebody says her name and the floor stays where it is." She turned toward the iron door. "Go and eat something. You look dreadful."

At the door, she stopped.

"For what it's worth, Mr. Calder," she said without turning around, "I've known Ansel Vey since he was nine years old. And he has never in his life used a word by accident."

Then she was gone.

---

Julian Vance was waiting for him in the courtyard.

It was nearly dusk. The lamps were coming on around the fountain, and most of the students had gone in to supper, but there were still a few dozen scattered around the benches and the steps. Owen came out of the clock tower with his head down, his book under his arm, and nearly walked into him.

"Calder," said Julian.

Owen stepped around him. Julian stepped in front of him again.

"Everyone's talking about you," Julian said. "Did you know that? The dock boy who floated the Weighing Stone. They're saying you're going to be the next great thing. They're saying the Ministry must have a very good reason to bring a Cable Street rat up the hill." He smiled. "I told them I know the reason."

"Move," said Owen.

"My father says the Ministry has a department," said Julian conversationally, "for dealing with *mistakes.* He wouldn't tell me which one. But he says every so often something comes out of the Bindery that shouldn't, and they take it somewhere underground, and it doesn't come back up." He tilted his head. "Except you came back up. So either you're not a mistake, or they haven't finished with you yet. Which is it, Calder?"

People had noticed. Owen could see them out of the corner of his eye, turning on the benches, drifting closer. A small circle was forming.

"Move," Owen said again.

"Or what?" Julian's hand came up, lazily, and a flame bloomed in his palm. Not a big one. Just big enough. It was white at the heart, the same white he'd burned the Weighing Stone with. "You'll do to me what you did to my cousin? In the middle of the courtyard? In front of *everyone?*"

The flame drifted closer to Owen's face. He could feel the heat on his cheek.

"You know what I think?" said Julian softly. "I think your father shovels coal because that's all your family's good for. I think your mother guts fish because that's all *she's* good for. And I think the only reason a dock rat like you is standing in the Aldren Institute is that somebody up here is scared of you. And I think that's the only thing you'll ever be good for, Calder. Being something people are *scared* of."

It happened very fast.

The hunger came up from the bottom of Owen like a wave coming over a pier. It didn't ask. It didn't whisper. It *rose*, and the gravel around his boots flattened into the ground in a perfect circle with a sound like a slap, and Julian's flame bent sideways and down and slammed into the stones at his feet as if it had been yanked on a string. Julian staggered. His knees buckled, just slightly, just enough, and the look on his face as the weight came down on him wasn't contempt anymore. It was fear.

And oh, it felt *good.*

*More,* said the hunger. *He's so small. He's so light. Press. Press him into the stones like his cousin. Let them all see.*

The circle of students had gone dead silent. The fountain had stopped flowing; the water in the basin was lying flat and heavy as glass.

Owen stood there with his book shut under his arm, breathing hard, and felt Julian Vance sinking toward the ground in front of him an inch at a time, and wanted it so badly he could taste it.

*If you hurt somebody we'd prefer you didn't hurt,* said Vey, in his head, *you'll go to sleep one night and simply not wake up.*

And under that, quieter, Kessler: *Out for six.*

Owen breathed out.

He breathed out for a long time. Longer than six. Longer than he'd known he could. He breathed out until his lungs burned and there was nothing left in him at all, and the hunger at the bottom of him howled with frustration and then, grudgingly, went still.

The weight lifted.

Julian stumbled backward, gasping. The fountain spluttered back to life. Somewhere across the courtyard, a door banged open, and there was a sharp crack of thunder out of a clear sky, and a voice yelled, "*Oi!*"

Jonah came across the courtyard at a run, in his brand-new storm-grey coat, with tiny blue sparks snapping off the ends of his fingers.

"What's going on?" He skidded to a stop between them. He looked from Owen to Julian and back again. His hair was standing straight up. "What's *going on?*"

"Nothing," said Owen. His voice came out rough. "Nothing's going on."

Julian was staring at him. He'd gone very white. He opened his mouth, and closed it again. Then he turned on his heel and walked away across the courtyard, fast, and the circle of watching students parted to let him through, and didn't close again behind him.

Jonah looked at Owen.

"Owen," he said quietly. "What was that?"

"Gravity," said Owen.

"That wasn't..." Jonah stopped. He looked down at the gravel, pressed flat in a perfect circle around Owen's boots, smooth as a pond. He looked at the fountain. He looked back up at Owen, and for the first time since they were ten years old, Owen saw his old friend look at him like he wasn't sure who he was.

"That wasn't the gas leak either, was it," Jonah said.

Owen didn't answer. He walked past him toward the dormitory, and didn't look back.

---

She found him on the roof.

He didn't know how she knew. The east dormitory had a flat roof above the top floor, reached by a narrow iron ladder and a trapdoor at the end of Owen's corridor, and he'd found it by accident on his way back from the washroom, and he'd climbed up because it was the only place in the whole Institute where nobody was looking at him. From up here you could see the whole city. The lamps coming on down the hill, street by street. The harbor, black, with the lights of the freighters on it. Cable Street, somewhere in that grey smudge by the cannery.

It was the same view he and Talia used to look up at, from the other end.

The trapdoor creaked. He didn't turn around.

"Go away," he said.

"No," said Talia.

She climbed out onto the roof. He heard her boots on the tar paper. He heard her stop, a few steps behind him. He could feel the warmth coming off her, even from there, like sitting too close to a stove.

"Owen," she said. "Please. I need to talk to you."

"You've had five months."

"I know."

"You had my whole birthday. You had the whole Bindery. You could have said something then, and instead you just stood there with your *handler.*" He heard his own voice going up and couldn't stop it. "You could've said something when they put the cuffs on me. Or when they put me in the car. Or last night, when you went to my *mother*, Talia, what did you tell her? What did they *tell* you to tell her?"

"Nobody told me anything!" Her voice cracked. "I went because she was *scared,* Owen, because nobody would tell her where you were, and I couldn't either, I didn't *know*..."

"You didn't know." He turned around. "You're a Grandmaster. You live next door to the Ministry. You sit on the headmistress's platform. And you didn't know that there's a place under the Bindery where they take kids like me and *kill them?*"

She went white.

"I didn't," she whispered. "Owen, I swear. I didn't know until..."

"Vey said you were useful."

"*Vey?*" Her whole face changed. "You met *Ansel Vey?*"

"He says you've been very useful to them."

"That's not..." She pressed both hands against her mouth. When she took them away, they were shaking. "Owen, I can't. I can't explain it. I'm not *allowed.* There's a rule. It's a stupid rule, and I hate it, and there are reasons for it I didn't even understand until last week, and if I break it, it's not me they'll..." She stopped. "It won't be me who gets hurt."

"That's convenient."

"*Please.*" She took a step toward him. "Please, just give me a few more months. Just until spring. And then I'll tell you everything, I swear it on Tess's cards, I'll tell you all of it, and you can hate me then if you still want to."

"I brought you a cake," said Owen.

She flinched as if he'd hit her.

"I spent every coin I had on it," he said. "I stood there on your landing, and you looked at me like I was nobody, and you shut the door. And I sat on your step all night. *All night,* Talia. Waiting for you to come back out." His voice was shaking so badly he could barely get the words out. "And you want me to wait until *spring?*"

He felt it then. The weight rising in him, hot and heavy, from somewhere much deeper than the hunger. Much older. The tar paper under his boots creaked and began to sag. The chimney stacks along the roof groaned. Down in the courtyard, far below, the water in the fountain went flat and still again.

Talia's light flared.

It came off her all at once, bright and white and gold, the way it had in the Bindery five months ago, as if some part of her had reacted before she could stop it. The whole rooftop lit up as bright as noon. Every shadow on it jumped away and vanished.

All except one.

Owen saw her see it. He saw her eyes go past his shoulder, to something behind him, and he saw her face change. Every bit of colour drained out of it. Her mouth fell open. She took a step back, and then another, until her heel was at the very edge of the trapdoor.

"Owen," she whispered. "Owen, don't move."

"What?"

"Behind you." Her voice was barely a breath. "There's something... behind you."

He turned.

There was nothing there. Just the chimney stacks, and the dark sky, and the lights of the city down the hill. His own shadow, stretched out long across the tar paper by her light.

He turned back.

"There's nothing..."

"It's *on* you." Talia's eyes were fixed on a point above his left shoulder, huge and horrified, and her light was flickering wildly around her. "It's standing right behind you. It's got its hand... Owen, it's got its hand on your shoulder. It's so *big.* It's got..." She pressed both hands over her mouth again. "It's got *horns.*"

The weight in Owen's chest went very still.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Talia's light guttered and dimmed, and whatever she'd seen, she stopped seeing it. Owen could tell by her face. The horror went out of it, and something else came in: something he'd never seen on Talia's face in the nine years he'd known her.

She was scared of him.

"Owen," she said. "What did they do to you?"

He didn't have an answer.

Talia looked at him for another long moment, with her hands pressed to her mouth. Then she turned, and climbed down through the trapdoor, and was gone.

Owen stood on the roof alone for a long time. Every so often he looked over his left shoulder. There was never anything there.

When he finally went down the ladder and back along the dark corridor to room nine, his book was heavier under his arm. He could feel it the whole way, dragging at him. When he got into the room and lit the little oil lamp on his desk and opened it, he saw why.

There was a second page.

He hadn't turned to it. He hadn't earned it. But there it was, freshly inked, the ink still gleaming wet on the paper as though someone had only just finished writing it. At the top, one word in dark letters:

**Hunger.**

And underneath, in smaller writing:

*Whatever you take in, you keep.*

He stared at it for a long time. He didn't know what it meant. He didn't want to know. He shut the book, and put it on the floor beside his bed, as far from his pillow as the room would let him.

---

Julian was already asleep in the bed by the window, or pretending to be, with his back very deliberately turned. There was a little flame burning in a glass jar on his nightstand, the kind rich kids used instead of oil lamps. It threw moving shadows over the ceiling.

Owen lay in the dark and watched them.

His head still ached from the trial. His whole body ached from the Deep Room. His throat ached from shouting at Talia. He could still feel the heat of Julian's flame on his cheek, and the look on Jonah's face in the courtyard, and the way Talia's voice had cracked in half when she said *horns*.

He lay very still and breathed. In for four. Out for six. In for four. Out for six.

He must have slept, because the next thing he knew, he was falling.

---

He was falling *up.*

That was the first thing he understood. He was falling, fast, the way you fall in dreams, with his stomach left somewhere far below, except that *below* was above him. He was falling toward the ceiling of room nine, and through it, and through the roof, and through the night sky above the Institute, and the stars were rushing past him *downward,* and the whole city was dropping away beneath his feet into the dark, and he couldn't breathe, and he couldn't scream, and he couldn't stop...

And then he landed.

Not hard. Not the way you land at the bottom of a fall. He landed the way you land in water, softly, sinking, until his feet found a floor.

He was standing in a hall.

It was the biggest room he had ever seen. Bigger than the Bindery. Bigger than the great hall of the Institute. It went on and on in every direction, into the dark, its ceiling lost somewhere so high up that he couldn't see it at all. And down the middle of it, running away from him so far that he couldn't see the end, stood a table.

It was a banquet table. The longest in the world. It was set for a feast, with a white cloth, and silver candlesticks, and plates, and goblets, and chairs, thousands and thousands of chairs, all pushed in neatly, all empty. The candles were burning. But their flames didn't go up. They pointed *down*, all of them, dripping toward the table, as if even the fire in here was being pulled.

And the plates were empty. Every single one. Thousands of silver plates, polished to a mirror shine, with nothing on them at all.

Owen stood at the near end of the table in his nightclothes, barefoot on a cold stone floor, and heard, from somewhere far away down the length of that endless table, the sound of someone laughing.

It was a woman's laugh. Low and rich and amused. It came closer, and closer, with no footsteps at all, until it seemed to be coming from right beside his ear.

"*Oh,*" said a voice. "*Oh, look at you. Look how thin you are.*"

He turned around.

She was sitting on the table.

She was sitting right on the white cloth among the silver plates, as if she'd been there all along. She was tall, taller than any woman he'd ever seen, even sitting down, and she was pale, so pale she almost glowed: a cold porcelain white with a faint grey-violet tint beneath it, like a doll that had been left too long in the dark. Her hair was a huge, wild cloud of pale cream curls, touched with lavender in the shadows, and it didn't hang down. It drifted around her head and shoulders, slow and weightless, the way hair floats underwater, as if gravity itself had given up on it. Two black horns curled up out of it, thick and ridged like a ram's, sweeping up and out and hooking back in at the tips.

Her face was the strangest part. It was smooth and still and perfect, like a painted mask, with a small, thin, calm smile. In the middle of her forehead was a mark of glowing gold: a crescent moon with a thin ring passing through it. After a moment Owen recognized it. It was the same mark that was stamped in black on the cover of his book.

At her throat was a black band with a stiff, ruffled collar, like the ruff in an old painting of a queen. Below that, a great gown fell from her neck to the table and spilled over its edge onto the floor. It was made of layer upon layer of soft, rounded, overlapping panels, like the feathers of some huge pale bird, ivory and lavender, scattered with tiny dark crescents. It covered everything. Only her hands came out of it, from wide feathered sleeves: long, pale grey fingers ending in long black claws.

Behind her, filling the dark above the table, were her wings.

They were enormous, far wider than she was tall, black-violet and jagged at the edges like torn paper. And they were covered in eyes. Dozens of them, painted there or grown there, almond-shaped and gold with dark pupils, and every single one of them was looking at him.

All around her, drifting through the air, coiling over her shoulders and looping lazily around the candlesticks, were long ribbons of *writing*: thick chains of cramped black handwriting, glowing faintly gold at the edges, as if someone had torn the lines out of an old book and let them float.

And her eyes...

They sat in rings of shadow, dark and plum-coloured, like a doll's painted eyes. The whites of them were black. Pure black, like the inside of a well. And in the middle of each one was an iris of burning gold, ringed with violet, fixed on him with an interest so bright and so total that he felt it on his skin, like heat.

She smiled.

She had a great many teeth.

"*There you are,*" she said. "*My boy. My little lightning rod. My hungry one.*"

Owen couldn't move. He couldn't make his legs work. He stood barefoot on the stone floor and stared up at her, and his heart was slamming so hard he thought it would burst.

"*You're Arcanula,*" he whispered.

"*Clever. He told you, did he? The pretty man with the apple?*" She tilted her head. Her hair drifted with the movement. "*He always did say it wrong. Too much fear in it. You say it better.*" She leaned down toward him from the table, until her face was very close, and she breathed in slowly through her nose, as if she was smelling something delicious. "*Mm. Say it again.*"

He didn't.

She laughed again, delighted, and straightened up.

"*Oh, you're stubborn. Good. I like stubborn. Stubborn lasts longer.*" She waved one clawed hand at the endless table, the thousands of empty chairs, the thousands of empty silver plates. "*Do you like my hall? I've been waiting in it for a very, very long time. All alone. No guests. No food.*" Her golden eyes came back to him. "*Sixty-one years, since the last time anyone was brave enough to open my book and let me eat. Can you imagine? Sixty-one years with nothing on my plate.*"

"What... what do you want?"

"*What does anyone want, darling?*" She smiled. "*I want to be* full."

She slid down off the table in one long, fluid movement, and suddenly she was standing in front of him, and she was tall, so tall he had to tilt his head right back to see her face. The soft pale layers of her gown spilled around his bare feet, cold as harbor water, and her wings rose up behind her until they blotted out the candles, and all their golden eyes looked down at him.

"*You fed me today,*" she murmured. "*Did you know? Just a little. Just a taste. When you lifted the pretty rock and held it up for all of them to see, and you felt them watching you. When you pressed that boy into the gravel, and you felt him get small, and you liked it.*" She reached out with one long claw and very gently touched the center of his chest. It was so cold it burned. "*And on the roof. With the girl. Oh, that was lovely. That was the first real meal I've had in sixty years.*"

"Stop," said Owen. His voice came out tiny.

"*I gave you a page for it,*" Arcanula went on, as if he hadn't spoken. "*Did you find it? My present? Whatever you take in, you keep.*" She smiled wider. "*That's the second thing we'll learn together, you and I. How to take. How to keep. How to swallow the whole world and never, ever be full.*"

And then, all at once, her face changed.

The smile went out of it like a candle blown out. Her golden eyes narrowed. Her curls stopped drifting and went flat and still around her head. Every eye on her wings narrowed with her. When she spoke again her voice was different: colder, much older, and very, very quiet.

"*He touched you.*"

Owen didn't understand. "What?"

"*The brute. Two doors down. The one who calls himself Wrath.*" Her claws came up and closed, very lightly, around the back of his neck. "*I can smell him on you. He put his hand on your shoulder in the cell. And again tonight, on the roof, while the girl watched. He stood behind you like he* owned *you.*"

Her grip tightened, not quite enough to hurt.

"*You are not his,*" said Arcanula. "*You were never his. You are* mine. *I woke for you. I chose you. I am the one who lives in your book and in your belly and in the dark at the bottom of you, and I will be the one who fills you up, page by page, until there's nothing left in this world you can't pull down. And when you finally walk into that cell of his, you'll walk in* full *of me. And we'll see how he likes being eaten.*"

For a moment she held him there, her burning golden eyes an inch from his, and Owen understood, with a certainty that went all the way to his bones, that she was the oldest thing he had ever met. Older than the Waste. Older than the old capital. Older than the city on the hill and the harbor below it and the first boat that ever tied up there. She had been hungry when the whole world was mud.

Then, just as suddenly, she let go, and laughed, and it was the playful laugh again, rich and warm and delighted, as if none of it had happened.

"*Oh, look at your face,*" she said. "*You're shaking. Poor thing. Poor little thing. You haven't had a proper meal in days, have you? Neither have I.*"

She turned, and her gown swept out behind her, and she walked away from him along the side of that endless table, trailing one claw along the white cloth so that it tore, very slightly, in a long thin line. The candles leaned toward her as she passed, their downward flames all bending her way.

Halfway along, she stopped, and pulled out one of the thousands of empty chairs. Then she turned and looked back at him over her shoulder, with her black-and-gold eyes, and her floating pale curls, and her black horns, and her wings full of watching eyes, and all her teeth.

"*Well?*" said Arcanula. "*Come here, Owen Calder. Sit down.*"

She patted the chair.

"*It's time we had dinner.*"

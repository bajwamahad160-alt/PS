# Chapter Four: A Seat at the Table

"No," said Owen.

His voice came out thin and small in that enormous hall, and it didn't echo. Nothing in here echoed. The sound just fell out of his mouth and dropped to the stone floor and lay there.

Arcanula tilted her head. Her black hair drifted with the movement, slow as weed in a tide.

"*No?*"

"I'm not sitting down." He made himself look up at her, into those black-and-gold eyes. "I'm not eating anything. I'm not doing anything you say. You're a..." He didn't know the word. "You're a *thing* that lives in a book. I don't have to do what you want."

For a moment she only looked at him. The candles along the endless table had all gone still, their downward flames hanging motionless, as if the whole hall were holding its breath.

Then she smiled, very slowly, with every one of her teeth.

"*Oh, darling,*" said Arcanula. "*You're standing in my stomach.*"

She crooked one long black claw.

The floor tipped.

It didn't tilt the way the Bindery had tilted, or the Deep Room. It *tipped*, all at once, as if the whole enormous hall had been a tray and somebody had picked up one end. Owen's bare feet went out from under him. He hit the stone on his side and slid, fast, across the cold floor toward her, and he grabbed at the legs of chairs as he went past and they didn't move, they were fixed there like iron posts, and his fingernails scraped on stone and found nothing to hold, and then the chair she was holding out was rushing up at him and he slammed into the seat of it so hard it knocked the breath out of him.

The floor tipped back. The chair scraped forward on its own and tucked him in against the table, neat as a waiter.

He couldn't get up. He tried. His arms and legs had gone heavy, so heavy he could barely lift his fingers off the white tablecloth. It was the same weight he'd poured out over the Bindery floor, the same weight that had pressed Julian toward the gravel. Now it was his turn to feel it, and it was *awful*. It pressed on his chest. It pressed on his eyelids. It held him in the chair like a hand.

"*There,*" said Arcanula, pleased, and sat down across from him. "*Much better. Nobody likes to eat alone.*"

She didn't pull out a chair. She simply sat, and one was there under her, a high carved thing black as her horns, and she leaned her elbows on the table and rested her chin on her folded claws and looked at him across the empty silver plate between them.

"*Now,*" she said. "*Let's talk about what's for dinner.*"

---

"*You've met the brute,*" said Arcanula.

It wasn't a question. Owen said nothing. He was concentrating on breathing. In for four. Out for six. It didn't seem to help much in here.

"*He's told you he chose you.*" She traced a slow circle on the tablecloth with one claw. "*He's told you he wants you to walk into his little cell and pick him up and carry him around forever. Has he told you why?*"

"He said he was bored."

Arcanula laughed. It was a real laugh, sudden and delighted, and the candle flames all shivered with it.

"*Bored. Oh, that's good. That's very good. I'll give him that one.*" She leaned closer. "*No, darling. He's not bored. He's* frightened."

"Of what?"

"*Of me.*"

She let that sit on the table between them like a dish.

"*Let me tell you a secret,*" she said. "*The pretty man with the apple doesn't know it. The old stone woman doesn't know it. Nobody in your whole city knows it, because nobody has lived long enough to watch it happen twice.*" She held up one claw. "*When one of the six fills their book and walks into that cell and picks up Reqrium, they don't go alone. We come with them. The demon in their book climbs up into the new one, like a crab moving into a bigger shell.*" A second claw. "*And the demon that was already sitting in Reqrium? He doesn't get to stay. He gets pushed out. He falls down into the empty book that was left behind, and he sits there, and he waits for some other child to come along and pick him up.*"

Owen stared at her.

"*So,*" said Arcanula softly, "*if the boy who takes Reqrium is carrying the Time book, then Sollumbre becomes Wrath, and the brute falls down to be Sloth. If it's the Mind book, then Visselth goes up, and he falls down to be Envy. And if it's* you, *my darling, if it's the Gravity book that walks into that cell...*"

She spread her claws, smiling.

"*Then I become Wrath. And Vorruk falls into my hall, to sit at my table, in front of my empty plates, and be* hungry *for sixty years.*"

The candles leaned toward her. Owen could feel his heart going very hard in his chest, under the weight.

"That's why he picked me," he said slowly. "He wants me because... because I've got *you*. So he can..."

"*So he can climb inside you first,*" said Arcanula. "*Yes. So that when you walk in, you'll already be his. So that when we come up the stairs together, he'll already be sitting in your chest, and there'll be no room for me, and he'll stay right where he is, on top, forever. That's what the* little room *was for, Owen. The one he asked you for. The one you said no to.*" She sat back. "*Clever boy. I told you I liked stubborn.*"

Owen's mouth was very dry.

"And you want the opposite," he said. "You want me to get there first, with you, so you can climb up and he falls down."

"*I want to be* full," said Arcanula. "*And there is nothing in this world more filling than Wrath.*"

---

"*So here's my offer,*" she said.

She snapped her claws. The silver plate in front of Owen, the one that had been empty, wasn't empty anymore. There were three things on it. He couldn't have said afterward what they looked like. They weren't food. They were more like *ideas* that had been given a shape and a shine and put on a plate, and when he looked at each one, he knew what it was the way you know things in dreams.

"*One,*" said Arcanula, and touched the first with a claw. "*Speed. You'll fill your book faster than any of the other five, because I'll help you. I know every page in it. I know what's written on the last one. I'll show you how to get there, one meal at a time.*"

"*Two.*" She touched the second. "*Him. The brute. While I'm awake inside you, he can't lay one finger on you. Not in the cell. Not on the roof. Not anywhere. I'll stand behind you, where he stood, and he'll have to go around.*"

"*And three.*" The third thing on the plate shone brighter than the others. "*The truth. About everything. The girl and her little locked door. The pretty man and his little lies. What the old stone woman really wants with you. I'll tell you all of it. Eventually.*"

"When?"

"*Dessert,*" said Arcanula, and smiled. "*Truth is always dessert.*"

"And what do you want for it?"

She didn't answer right away. She looked at him across the table, and for once there was no playfulness in her face at all. Just hunger. Old and patient and bottomless.

"*Now and then,*" she said quietly, "*I'd like to go for a walk.*"

He didn't understand at first. Then he did, and the cold went all the way down his back.

"*Not often,*" said Arcanula. "*Not for long. An hour here. An afternoon there. I've been sitting at this table in the dark for sixty-one years, darling. I'd like to feel the sun. I'd like to taste something that isn't the inside of a book.*" She tilted her head. "*With your mouth. With your hands. In your shoes. And then I'll give them back, a little more full than I found them.*"

"You want to *be* me."

"*Only sometimes.*"

"No."

"*No?*" Her eyebrows went up. "*Then say no, and wake up, and go back to your nice new bed and your nice new school, and see how long you last. See how long you last when the brute comes back tonight, and tomorrow night, and every night after that, standing behind you on every roof with his hand on your shoulder, whispering about that girl. See how long it takes him to find his little room.*" She leaned in. "*You felt him on the roof, didn't you? The weight that came up out of you when the girl said* spring. *That wasn't me, darling. That was* him. *And he'd only just started.*"

Owen didn't say anything.

"*Or,*" said Arcanula, and her voice went soft again, almost gentle, "*you could say yes. And I'll stand between you and him. And you'll go faster than all of them. And one day you'll walk into that cell and pick up the whole world, and he'll fall down here into the dark where he belongs. And you'll only have lent me your shoes once in a while.*"

She held out her hand across the table. Long grey fingers. Black claws. The thin gold crescents on her wrist glowing faintly.

"*Well?*"

Owen looked at her hand for a long time.

He thought about the corner of cell four, and the dark folding against the ceiling like smoke. He thought about Talia's face on the roof, gone white, her hands over her mouth. *It's got its hand on your shoulder.* He thought about Vey's form with his address typed on it. He thought about five other kids, somewhere out there, filling their books.

He thought about his mother saying: *Things you leave too long get heavy.*

"Only sometimes," he said. "An hour. Not more. And you give it back."

"*Only sometimes,*" Arcanula agreed.

"And you don't hurt anyone. When you've got my hands. You don't hurt my family, or Jonah, or..." He stopped. "Or anyone."

Arcanula smiled.

"*I won't hurt anyone you love,*" she said.

He knew, even then, that it wasn't the same thing. He knew it the way you know a step is missing in the dark. But the weight on his chest was so heavy, and she was so close, and the thought of Vorruk standing behind him every night was so much worse.

He lifted his hand off the tablecloth. It took everything he had. And he put it in hers.

Her claws closed around it. They were so cold they burned, like the Binders had burned, like iron in winter, and something went into his palm from hers, a sharp bright sting, like a splinter of ice going in under the skin.

"*There,*" whispered Arcanula. "*Now we're family.*"

And the whole hall fell *upward*, and the candles went out, and Owen woke up in room nine with a gasp, in the grey light of dawn, soaked in sweat and clutching his right hand against his chest.

He opened it.

In the middle of his palm, faint and silvery, like an old scar that had been there for years, was the shape of a crescent moon.

---

Julian's bed was empty and neatly made. The flame in the glass jar on his nightstand had gone out.

Owen sat on the edge of his own bed in the grey light and stared at the mark on his palm until the breakfast bell rang. It didn't hurt. It didn't do anything. It just sat there, pale and quiet, the way the hunger sat at the bottom of him, and when he closed his fingers over it, he could feel, very faintly, something like a smile.

*Good morning, darling,* said a voice in the back of his head. *I'm starving.*

He went down to breakfast.

The dining hall at the Aldren Institute was a long, high room with tall windows and six long tables, and it smelled of coffee and toast and floor polish. Owen took his tray to the far end of the last table, where nobody was sitting, and nobody came to sit near him. That was fine. He could hear them anyway. *Bindery boy.* *Gas leak.* *Julian's roommate. Where's Julian?* The whispers slid along the tables and around him like water around a stone.

He ate two bowls of porridge. Then he went back up and got a third.

He ate four slices of toast, and three boiled eggs, and a sausage somebody had left on the serving tray, and a heel of bread he found at the bottom of the basket. He ate until his stomach hurt. It didn't help. The food went down into him and vanished, the way a stone vanishes into a well, and the empty place at the bottom of him stayed exactly as empty as it had been.

And it wasn't only the food.

He noticed it halfway through the third bowl. A first-year girl two tables down had dropped her cup, and it had smashed, and she'd gone bright red, and for one second Owen had *felt* it. Her embarrassment. It had come across the room to him warm and sharp, like the smell of something frying, and the hunger at the bottom of him had lifted its head and *sniffed* at it.

He put his spoon down.

He looked along the tables, at all the whispering faces, and he felt them. Not their words. The things underneath. Curiosity, bright and thin as sugar. Fear, darker and richer. A boy by the window who was nervous about an exam, and a girl by the door who was homesick, and somebody, somewhere, who was very, very angry about something, and all of it was drifting toward him on the warm air of the dining hall like steam off a kitchen, and all of it smelled *good*.

*Isn't it lovely?* murmured Arcanula. *All these little dishes. And nobody's even touching them.*

Owen pushed his tray away, and breathed out for a long time, and walked out of the dining hall with his hands in his pockets, and was still hungry when the bell went for lessons.

---

The first lesson of the morning was Grandmaster Lore, and it was taught by the thin teacher with the creaking-door voice, whose name turned out to be Master Quill.

It was held in a long, sunny classroom on the second floor of the north wing, with rows of old wooden desks and a huge painted map of the country on one wall. The first-years sat at the desks. At the front, on a separate bench, sat the handful of students who wore white or grey coats instead of charcoal: the Grandmasters, who apparently took Lore with everyone else, because, as Master Quill said, "a Grandmaster who does not know their own history is merely a very loud accident."

Talia was on the bench. So was Jonah, looking uncomfortable in his new storm-grey coat, with his knees up around his ears because the bench was too low for him.

Owen took a desk at the very back.

He'd barely sat down when the chair beside him scraped, and Jonah dropped into it, having apparently climbed over two rows of desks to get there.

"You're supposed to sit on the bench," Owen said.

"The bench is for people with short legs." Jonah stretched his out into the aisle with a sigh of relief. "And people who like being looked at. Talia's on it. That's two strikes." He glanced sideways at Owen. "You look terrible, by the way."

"Thanks."

"No, I mean it. You look like you've been up all night fighting a bear." Jonah frowned. "What's wrong with your hand?"

Owen closed his fist. "Nothing."

Jonah looked at him for a second. Then, to Owen's surprise, he let it go. He just nodded, and turned to face the front, and opened his textbook, and Owen felt something in his chest unclench very slightly that he hadn't even known was clenched.

"*The Seven,*" said Master Quill, and tapped the blackboard with a long pointer. "*A History.* Chapter One. Open your books."

The textbook was old and thick and bound in green cloth, with the seven Grandmaster marks stamped in gold on the cover. Owen opened it to the first page, and there was a picture there, a full-page engraving of the Bindery ceiling: seven marks in a ring, flame and water and air and earth and light and ice and thunder, each one in its own careful circle.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked closer.

The ring wasn't a ring. Not really. The seven marks were spaced out evenly all the way around, but between each pair of them there was a gap. A gap exactly the size of another mark. And in each gap, if you tilted the page toward the window and let the morning light slide across it, you could see that the engraving had been scraped. Very carefully. Somebody, a long time ago, had taken a fine blade to the printing plate, and cut something away, seven times, and filled the spaces in with a pattern of little stars.

Owen ran his thumb over one of the gaps. The paper was smooth. You'd never have known, if you hadn't seen a stone corridor with seven shapes gouged out of its walls.

"*Mr. Calder.*"

He looked up. Master Quill was staring at him down the length of the classroom. So was everyone else.

"You seem very absorbed in the frontispiece," said Master Quill. "Perhaps you could tell us what you find so fascinating about it."

The room had gone quiet. Owen could feel them all waiting. *The Bindery boy. The gas leak.*

"There's gaps," he said. "Between the marks. Like something's missing."

For just a second, something moved in Master Quill's face. Then it was gone.

"Decorative spacing," said Master Quill crisply. "The engraver's choice. Page three, everyone, please. The Founding of the Flame."

And he turned back to the blackboard. But Owen saw his hand, holding the chalk, and it was not quite steady.

The rest of the lesson was the Founding. Master Quill told it the way he must have told it a hundred times, in his creaking-door voice, pacing up and down in front of the painted map: how the seven great books had first been found, long ago, in the time before the old capital; how the first Grandmasters had carried them into the wars and out again; how a Grandmaster book never dies and never sleeps, but only waits, when its holder goes, for the next sixteen-year-old hand to touch it.

"Which is why," said Master Quill, "the passing of a Grandmaster is both a funeral and a birth. Mr. Pike can tell us something about that, I imagine."

Jonah, who had been drawing a very small storm cloud in the margin of his textbook, sat up fast. "Uh. Yes, sir. It's... it's weird, sir."

"Eloquently put." Master Quill made a note on the board. "*Weird.* I shall submit it to the Royal Society."

Everyone laughed. Even Owen, a little. It felt strange in his mouth, like a word in a language he'd forgotten.

He didn't hear most of what came after. He sat with his thumb on the scraped gap in the engraving and let Master Quill's voice go past him. Seven gaps. Seven stars. Seven marks cut out of a printing plate by someone with a very steady hand, a very long time ago, so that a whole country could open its schoolbooks every morning for a hundred years and never once wonder what was missing.

*They were very thorough,* said Arcanula, in the back of his head. *I'll give them that. They were very, very thorough.* A pause. *It didn't help them in the end.*

On the bench at the front, Talia had turned around. She was looking straight at Owen down the length of the room, over the heads of all the other students, and her face was very serious, and he thought, for one strange moment, that she was about to say something.

Then her eyes moved. Just slightly. Up, and to the left, to a point above his shoulder.

He watched the colour drain out of her face the way it had on the roof. He watched her lips go white. She turned around so fast that she knocked her textbook off the bench, and it hit the floor with a slap, and Master Quill said "*Miss Reyes,*" in a pained voice, and she bent to pick it up with shaking hands and didn't turn around again for the rest of the lesson.

*She can see me,* said the voice in the back of Owen's head, delighted. *Oh, isn't that sweet. The little lamp can see me.*

Owen stared at the back of Talia's head, and the gold ribbon in her hair, and felt sick.

---

At lunch, a boy two tables over was reading a newspaper, and he read the headline out loud to his friends as a joke, in a spooky voice.

"*WHOLE VILLAGE SLEEPS THREE DAYS,*" he intoned. "*Marrowford Residents Wake to Stopped Clocks, Missing Time.*" He grinned. "Listen to this. *Every one of the three hundred residents of the farming village of Marrowford, forty miles north of Port Aldren, fell asleep on the evening of the fourteenth and did not wake until the morning of the seventeenth. All clocks in the village were found stopped at eleven minutes past nine. Livestock, too, appear to have slept. Doctors are baffled.*" He wiggled his fingers. "Ooooh. *Baffled.*"

His friends laughed.

Owen had stopped eating.

*Time is Sloth,* Vey had said, ticking it off on his fingers. *And its demon is called Sollumbre.*

*Wanting to stop the clock,* he thought, *and never have to move.*

Beside him, Jonah was laughing too. Then he noticed Owen's face, and stopped.

"What?" he said. "It's just some farmers having a lie-in."

"Yeah," said Owen. "Probably."

But under the table, he closed his hand over the crescent on his palm, and he felt, very distinctly, Arcanula go still inside him. The way a cat goes still when it hears something in the walls.

*Sollumbre,* she said softly. *Lazy old thing. Awake already.* A pause. *Forty miles. That's not very far, darling. Not very far at all.*

---

In the Deep Room that afternoon, Kessler made him breathe for an hour, and then made him lighten a boulder the size of a cart, and then made him put it down again very slowly, and then made him do it all again.

Halfway through the third time, she stopped walking.

"Show me your right hand," she said.

Owen went still. The boulder hung in the air above him.

"Put the rock down, Mr. Calder. Slowly. Then show me your hand."

He put the rock down. He held out his hand, palm up, because there didn't seem to be any point in doing anything else.

Kessler looked at the crescent for a long time. She didn't touch it. Her cliff of a face didn't move. But the stone floor of the Deep Room, all around them, made a very small sound, like something heavy settling.

"When?" she said.

"Last night."

"In a dream."

"Yes."

"And she dragged you to the table, and you said no, and it didn't matter, and then she offered you everything you wanted, and asked for something that didn't sound too bad." Kessler closed her eyes briefly. "And you said yes."

Owen stared at her. "How do you...?"

"Every one of the four made a deal, Mr. Calder." Kessler opened her eyes. "Every single one. They all had a mark, somewhere, afterward. One on the wrist. One on the back of the neck. One behind the ear, under her hair, where she thought nobody would see." She looked at his palm. "Not one of them ever told me what they'd agreed to. Not until it was far too late."

She was quiet for a moment.

"What did you promise her, Mr. Calder?"

Owen closed his hand.

He thought about telling her. He wanted to, in a strange way. Kessler was the only person in the whole city who seemed to understand what was happening to him, and she was standing right there, small and upright and grey, waiting. It would be easy. *She wants to go for a walk. Sometimes. An hour.* It sounded like nothing, said out loud. It sounded almost sweet.

And at the bottom of him, very gently, like a finger laid against his lips, something said: *Shh.*

"Nothing much," said Owen.

Kessler looked at him for a long time.

"That," she said, "is what the one with the mark behind her ear told me. Word for word." She turned her face away, toward the far wall of the Deep Room, where the lamplight didn't quite reach. "She was fifteen days from her seventeenth birthday. She liked oranges. She used to peel them in one long curl and give me the peel, because she said it smelled like summer." Her voice didn't change at all. "I kept one, for a while. In my desk. It went hard and brown, and one day I threw it away."

Owen didn't know what to say. So he didn't say anything.

"Again," said Kessler, without turning around. "The boulder. And this time, I want you to breathe out for eight."

---

The sparring class was held in the old armory, a long, low stone building on the west side of the courtyard with sand on the floor and racks of padded practice staves along the walls. It was taught by a huge, bald, scarred man named Master Brand, who had been in the army and still walked like it, and who had apparently only one rule, which he shouted at them as they came in:

"*Nobody dies on my sand!*"

He paired them off by pulling names out of a leather bag. The first pairs were ordinary: a boy with air against a girl with water, a strength kid against a metal kid. They circled each other on the sand while Master Brand bellowed advice, and nobody got hurt worse than a few bruises.

Then Master Brand pulled out a slip of paper, looked at it, and paused.

"Vance," he said. "And..." He looked at the next slip. "Calder."

Owen looked across the sand. Julian Vance was already looking back at him. He'd been looking at Owen since they walked in. There was no expression on his face at all, but his hands were curled into fists at his sides, and small wisps of smoke were rising from between his fingers.

"Sir," said Julian. "I'd like to go first."

"You're both going first, Vance. That's how pairs work." Master Brand stepped back to the edge of the sand. "Standard rules. Three points. First to a clean touch, or a yield. *Nobody dies on my sand.* Begin."

They circled.

Julian struck first. It was a clean, fast, textbook shot, a spear of orange fire as thick as a broom handle, aimed low at Owen's legs, and Owen didn't think. He said "*Lighten,*" to himself, to his own body, the way Kessler had made him practice a hundred times on the boulder, and the sand dropped away beneath his boots.

He went up. Not far. Maybe four feet. The fire went under him and splashed against the far wall in a shower of sparks, and he hung there for one long, astonished second, weightless, with his coat floating out around him, and then he remembered that he hadn't planned how to come down and came down all at once, sideways, and landed on his hip in the sand with a grunt.

Somebody laughed. Master Brand didn't.

"*Good!*" he roared. "Ugly as sin, Calder, but good! You're alive, aren't you? Being alive is the whole point! Again!"

Owen got up, spitting sand. Across the circle, Julian's face had gone very still.

He still had his book open in his left hand. He still had no idea what he was doing. He'd never fought anyone in his life except Jonah, when they were eight, over a plum, and Jonah had won by sitting on him. He watched Julian's hands, and his feet, and the smoke curling up off his knuckles, and he breathed. In for four. Out for eight.

"Everyone's still talking about the courtyard," said Julian quietly, as they circled. Too quietly for Master Brand to hear. "Did you know? They say you made me kneel."

"I didn't make you do anything."

"*They say you made me kneel.*" Julian's voice cracked, just a little, on the last word. "In front of the whole school. My father heard about it last night. He telephoned. Do you know what he said to me?" His face twisted. "He asked me what a *Vance* was doing on his knees in front of a *dock rat.*"

"Julian..."

"*Don't* say my name."

And he threw the fire.

It wasn't a sparring throw. It wasn't a touch. It was everything he had: a roaring torrent of white-hot flame, pure and blinding, the same fire that had turned the Weighing Stone white. It came across the sand straight at Owen's chest, and the air around it rippled and screamed, and somewhere Master Brand was bellowing "*VANCE!*" and somewhere else someone was screaming, and there was no time to lighten anything, no time to breathe, no time to do anything at all...

Except open his hand.

He didn't decide to. It just happened. His right hand came up, palm out, crescent out, the way you'd throw up your hand to stop a ball hitting you in the face. And the word came up out of the second page of his book and out of his mouth on its own:

"*Hunger.*"

The fire went in.

It didn't hit him. It didn't burn him. It poured into the crescent on his palm like water pouring down a drain, all of it, the whole white roaring torrent, and it kept pouring, and pouring, and it didn't stop until the last flicker of it had gone. Then the air rippled once more, and cleared.

The armory went dark.

Not completely. But every lamp along the walls had gone out at the same moment, their flames sucked sideways and down into Owen's hand along with Julian's fire, and the whole long room was suddenly dim and cold, lit only by the grey afternoon light coming through the high windows.

Owen stood on the sand with his hand still up.

He could feel it. The fire. It was *inside* him. Not burning. Sitting. A hot, bright weight in the middle of his chest, like a coal from Pop's boilers that he'd swallowed whole, and he could feel it there, humming, waiting, *his.* He could feel that if he wanted to, if he just opened his hand again and *let go*, it would come back out of him. All of it. White and roaring and twice as hot.

*Whatever you take in,* said the second page of his book, *you keep.*

And at the bottom of him, low and warm and utterly satisfied, like someone sinking into a hot bath after a long day, Arcanula sighed.

*Oh,* she breathed. *Oh, that was* delicious.

Julian Vance was on his knees on the sand.

Nobody had pushed him. He'd just gone down. His hands were empty and shaking, and there was no smoke coming off them now, and his face, in the grey light, was the colour of paper. He was staring at Owen's raised hand. He was staring at the crescent on it.

"What *are* you?" he whispered.

Nobody else in the armory said a word. Master Brand was standing at the edge of the sand with his mouth open. The other students were pressed back against the weapon racks. Somewhere at the back, a girl had started to cry.

Owen lowered his hand.

He could still feel the fire in his chest. He could still feel Arcanula, purring. And he could feel, right at the very back of his mind, a thought that wasn't his, sweet and patient: *Give it back to him. Go on. He threw it at you. He'd have burned your face off. Give it* back.

He closed his hand into a fist.

"Yield," he said.

The word came out hoarse. Master Brand blinked at him.

"What?"

"I yield." Owen turned around and walked off the sand. "He wins. Point to Vance."

He didn't look back. He walked out of the armory into the grey afternoon with his fist clenched so tight that his nails cut into his palm, and the fire sat in his chest like a stone, and Arcanula laughed softly at the back of his mind all the way across the courtyard.

*You're no fun,* she said fondly. *But you're learning.*

He went to the washroom in the east dormitory, because it was the only place he could think of where nobody would be, and he locked himself in the end stall and sat on the closed lid with his head in his hands.

The fire was still there. It wasn't going anywhere. He could feel the exact shape of it in his chest, hot and bright and roughly the size of a fist, and when he breathed in, it flared, and when he breathed out, it dimmed, like a coal under a bellows. He breathed out for eight. Then for ten. It dimmed, but it didn't go out.

After a while he went to the basin and splashed cold water on his face, and when he looked up at the little square mirror, he saw that the gas lamp on the wall beside it had bent.

Its flame wasn't pointing up anymore. It was leaning sideways, out of its glass, toward him. Straining toward him, thin and blue, the way a plant in a cellar strains toward the one crack of light. When he took a step to the left, it followed. When he took a step to the right, it followed.

*It knows where the rest of it went,* said Arcanula, amused. *Fire's like that. It always wants to go home.*

Owen turned the lamp off at the tap, and stood in the dark washroom, breathing, until his hands stopped shaking.

---

Jonah found him on the roof again, just after sunset.

He came up through the trapdoor with his hair sticking up and his storm-grey coat buttoned wrong, and he had something in each fist. He sat down on the tar paper next to Owen, with his long legs stuck out over the edge of the roof, and held out one hand.

It was a plum.

Owen looked at it.

"Where'd you get that?"

"Kitchens." Jonah shrugged. "They've got a whole barrel. Nobody was watching it. Old habits." He was still holding it out. "Go on. It's not poisoned. I'm Thunder, not whatever the poison one is."

Owen took the plum.

They sat and ate them in silence, looking out over the city as the lamps came on, street by street, down the hill to the harbor. When they'd finished, Jonah leaned forward and spat his stone off the edge of the roof, into the dark. After a moment, Owen did the same. They listened. Somewhere far below, very faintly, there was a *tick* as one of them hit the courtyard gravel.

"Mine went farther," said Jonah.

"It did not."

"It absolutely did. Mine hit the fountain. I heard it splash."

"You didn't hear anything."

"I heard it with my *heart*, Owen." Jonah grinned. "Anyway, I've got a proud history with plums. Remember Mrs. Abernathy's tree? When we were eight?"

"You sat on me."

"I *won*. Fair and square. You said the big one was yours, and I said it was mine, and then I sat on you until you agreed it was mine." Jonah looked enormously pleased with himself. "Best fight I ever had. Undefeated."

"You weighed twice what I did."

"That's called *strategy*." Jonah leaned back on his elbows. "And then Mrs. Abernathy came out with a broom and chased us both all the way down Cable Street, and you fell in the gutter, and your mom made us both go and say sorry, and Mrs. Abernathy gave us the plums anyway." He was smiling at the sky. "That was a good day."

"Yeah," said Owen. It had been. He'd forgotten. He hadn't let himself remember much about Cable Street, the last few months. It had been easier to keep it all shut up in a box, the way you'd keep a burn out of the air. "That was a good day."

Then Jonah's smile faded, and he was quiet for a while. When he spoke again, he didn't look at Owen. He looked out at the harbor.

"I heard about the armory," he said.

Owen didn't say anything.

"Everyone's heard about the armory. They're saying you ate Julian Vance's fire. They're saying the lamps went out." Jonah picked at the tar paper with his thumbnail. "They're saying a lot of stuff. Some of it's pretty bad."

"Some of it's probably true."

Jonah was quiet again. Then he said, "I'm not gonna ask."

Owen looked at him.

"I'm not," said Jonah. "I want to. I really, really want to. It's killing me, Owen. But I figure if you could tell me, you would've told me, and you haven't, so you can't. So I'm not gonna ask." He turned and looked at Owen properly for the first time. "I just... I'm gonna say one thing, and then I'll shut up, alright?"

"Alright."

"Don't disappear on me again." Jonah's voice was very steady. "That's all. After Talia left, you just... went. You stopped coming up on the roof. You stopped answering the door. I knocked for three months, Owen. *Three months.* And then I figured you didn't want me knocking. So I stopped." He swallowed. "And I've felt rotten about it every single day since. So whatever this is, whatever's going on with you and the gas leak and the fire and the thing with your hand, I don't need to know what it is. I just need you to not disappear. Okay?"

Owen looked at his old friend, sitting on the edge of the roof with his hair on end and his coat buttoned wrong and a plum stain on his chin, and something in his throat went very tight.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay." Jonah let out a long breath, and grinned. "Good. Great. That was really awkward. I'm glad it's over." He stood up and brushed tar off his coat. "Same time tomorrow? I'll bring more plums. I'm gonna rob that barrel blind."

"Same time tomorrow," said Owen.

Jonah climbed down through the trapdoor. Owen heard him on the ladder, humming. Then the humming stopped, and there was a pause, and then Jonah's voice, a bit awkward: "Oh. Hi. Um. He's... he's up there."

And then someone else was climbing up the ladder.

---

It was Talia.

She came up through the trapdoor slowly, and she didn't come all the way. She stopped with her head and shoulders out on the roof and her hands gripping the edges of the hatch, and she didn't look at him. She was looking down, at the tar paper, very carefully. Her eyes were fixed on a spot about a yard in front of his boots.

"Don't," said Owen.

"I'm not going to look at you," said Talia. Her voice was shaking. "I'm not. I'm going to keep my eyes on the roof, and I'm not going to look up, and I'm going to say one thing. Just one. And then I'll go. Please."

Owen didn't say anything. He didn't say yes. He didn't say no.

She took that as an answer.

"That night," she said, to the tar paper. "On the landing. With the cake." Her hands were white on the edges of the hatch. "I didn't shut the door because I wanted to. I've never wanted anything *less* in my whole life. I shut it because they told me it was the only way to keep you safe, and I believed them, and I..." She stopped. Breathed. "And I think I might have been wrong. About some of it. And I think it might be too late now. But I needed you to know that it was never because I didn't..."

She stopped again.

And then, before she could stop herself, she looked up.

Owen watched it happen. He watched her eyes lift from the tar paper to his face, and then past his face, up and to the left, to the place just over his shoulder. And he watched her whole face go still and white and horrified, exactly the way it had in the classroom, exactly the way it had the night before.

Only this time she didn't scream. She didn't flinch. She just stared, very quietly, at whatever was standing behind him.

"It's different," she whispered.

"What?"

"Last night it was... big. Dark. It had horns, and it was so *big.*" Her voice was barely a breath. "This one's a woman. She's got her arms around you. Her chin's on your shoulder. And she's..." Talia's lips were trembling. "She's *smiling* at me, Owen. She's got so many teeth."

Owen didn't turn around. He didn't need to. He could feel it: the cold, fond weight of something resting against his back, and a sharp chin on his shoulder, and a voice murmuring into his ear so softly that only he could hear it.

*Tell her to run along, darling,* said Arcanula. *Tell her the lamp is making my eyes hurt.*

"Go, Talia," Owen said.

She looked at him. Just at him, for one second. Her golden eyes were wet.

"Keep me safe from what?" Owen said. "You said they told you it would keep me safe. Safe from *what?*"

But she was already gone, down through the trapdoor, and he heard her boots on the ladder, fast, and then running down the corridor below, and then nothing.

---

When he came down off the roof, room nine was empty. Julian's things were gone. The bed by the window had been stripped to the mattress, and the wardrobe door hung open on nothing, and there was a note on the desk in beautiful handwriting that said only: *Transferred to the west dormitory at my father's request. Do not attempt to contact me.* It wasn't signed.

Owen sat down on his own bed.

He could still feel the fire in his chest. It hadn't faded. It sat there, hot and bright and patient, like a coal at the bottom of a stove that just needed one breath of air.

His book lay beside him. He hadn't opened it since the armory. He opened it now.

The first page: **Gravity.** *Lighten. Press. Pull.*

The second page: **Hunger.** *Whatever you take in, you keep.*

And a third page. Brand new. The ink still wet.

**Feast.**

*Whatever you keep, you become.*

He read it three times. He didn't understand it. And then, slowly, he did, and he sat very still on the bed with the book open on his knees and the fire burning in his chest, and he thought about the white flame, *Julian's* flame, that was sitting inside him right now. He thought about what it would mean to *become* it. To keep it. To make it his.

To open his hand, one day, and have it come out *his*.

And then he thought about what else a person could take in, and keep, if they were hungry enough.

He shut the book.

*Don't look so worried,* said Arcanula, from somewhere just behind his ear. Her voice was lazy and warm and very full. *It's only the third course. There's so much more to come.* A pause, and then, softer, almost shy: *And soon, darling, very soon now, it'll be my turn to go for a walk. You promised. Remember?*

"I remember," said Owen, to the empty room.

*Good boy,* she murmured, and went quiet.

He turned out the lamp and lay down on his back in the dark, with his right hand closed tight over the crescent, and the fire in his chest, and the book on the floor beside the bed, and he breathed. In for four. Out for eight. In for four. Out for eight.

Outside, the clock tower struck midnight.

On the eleventh stroke, the air in the middle of room nine *split open*.

---

It made no sound. That was the worst part. It just happened: a thin, bright line appeared in the empty air above Julian's stripped mattress, as if someone had drawn it there with a knife. It was perfectly straight and perfectly thin and as long as a door is tall. And then it *opened*, the two sides of it peeling back like the edges of a torn page, and through it Owen could see somewhere else.

Somewhere that wasn't room nine. Somewhere cold, and dark, and blue, with snow falling in it, and a sound like wind moving over a long way of nothing at all.

He couldn't move. He lay flat on his bed with his heart slamming and stared at the tear in the air.

A hand came through it.

It was a pale hand, slim, with long fingers, and a ring on every one of them. Silver rings, gold rings, rings with stones in them, rings of plain black iron, crowded so thick on each finger that they clinked together as the hand moved. It reached into the room, slowly, and it turned over, palm up, as if feeling the air for rain.

Then it stopped. As if it had felt something.

As if it had felt *him*.

"*Found you,*" said a voice, from the other side of the tear.

It was a young voice. A girl's voice, maybe, or a boy's; it was hard to tell. It sounded pleased. It sounded like someone who had been looking for a coin down the back of a very large sofa for a very long time, and had just, finally, touched the edge of it with their fingertips.

"*Gravity,*" said the voice, and laughed. "*Oh, the last one. The very last one. They hid you so nicely.*"

The ringed fingers curled, slowly, into a fist.

"*See you soon,*" said the voice.

And the hand drew back through the tear, and the tear sealed itself up like a healing cut, and room nine was dark and empty and silent again, as if nothing had ever been there at all.

Owen lay on his bed and didn't breathe.

Deep inside him, under the fire, under the hunger, he felt Arcanula come fully awake. He felt her rise up, slow and huge and cold, to her full height inside his chest. He felt her hair stop drifting. He felt every one of her teeth.

*Space,* she hissed. *Greedy little thing. Karvesh's pet.*

And then, softer, in a voice he had never heard her use before, a voice that was almost like a promise:

*Oh, darling. Dinner's coming to us.*
